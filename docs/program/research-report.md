# Progress a hundred movements with simple rules

The research behind strength programming comes down to a few solid findings and a lot of convention, and Javier's plan already rests mostly on the solid part. Progression driven by performance (double progression, RIR- or RPE-based loading, APRE) is **at least as good as fixed-percentage programming and possibly slightly better**. The pooled advantage is roughly 0–5 kg over 6–10 weeks, and the most careful meta-analysis finds no significant difference ([Hickmott et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9); [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)). Strength needs **little volume per movement**: one hard set a week already registers, 2–4 sets a week is the efficient zone, and gains stop growing detectably at about 4–5 weekly sets or about 2 direct sets per session ([Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908); [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)). But strength is **specific**: each of his ~100 movements needs its own direct sets, so the limit on a "no weak links" program is time, not physiology. How close a set goes to failure barely matters for strength ([Robinson et al. 2024](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654)). The specific numbers every program uses (increment sizes, "3 stalls then −10%", deload length, structural-balance ratios) are coaching convention or folklore, not tested results. The biggest unknown unknown is **measurement noise**. A true 1RM varies about 4% from test to test ([Grgic et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z)), and improvised isometric tests vary about 15–20% between sessions ([PMC9031103](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9031103)). So a "weak link" can only be judged from **trends over 8–12 exposures, scaled to each movement's noise**, never from a single comparison and not from Poliquin-style ratios. In practice: keep the queue, double progression, RIR logging, reactive deloads and L/R logging. Add an RIR gate and equipment-aware increments to the progression rule. Replace the return-ramp baseline with an automatic rolling rule. Demote reference ratios to hints, except left:right. Accept that some edge movements (scapular tilt, toe abduction, tibial rotation) are skills, not strength qualities.

A note on sources: PubMed, PMC and Europe PMC were largely blocked during the research. Several numbers therefore come from publisher pages, repositories, preprints or reputable secondary summaries, and this is flagged where it matters. Claims the researchers could not verify are listed separately near the end and are not used as evidence.

## Nine ideas explain most of strength programming

Someone coming to this field from software meets a vocabulary that mixes physiology, statistics and gym tradition. The useful move is to know which terms carry evidence. The table below is a map of the territory. The rest of the report fills in the numbers.

| Concept | Plain meaning | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| **1RM / e1RM** | The heaviest single rep possible. e1RM estimates it from a submaximal set with a formula such as Epley, load × (1 + reps/30). | 1RM tests are very reliable (median CV ~4%). Formula estimates are good up to ~5 reps, acceptable to ~10, and poor beyond that ([Reynolds et al. 2006](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)). |
| **RIR / RPE** | Reps in reserve: how many more reps you could have done. RPE 10 = 0 RIR, RPE 9 = 1 RIR, and so on. | Validated against bar speed ([Zourdos et al. 2016](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/efef3b25-6701-4fb5-bb82-55fcd2a26027/full)). People misjudge it by about 1 rep on average ([Halperin et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)). |
| **Progressive overload models** | Rules for when to add load: linear (every session), double progression (reps first, then load), %1RM tables, APRE (performance on a hard set sets the next load), RPE-based, velocity-based (VBT). | All work. Autoregulated models are ≥ fixed % (meta-analyses). The parameters of each are convention. |
| **Autoregulation** | Adjusting load or volume to today's performance instead of a preset plan. | Meta-analytic support, small effect. |
| **Periodization** | Planned variation over time: linear, undulating (DUP, changing reps from day to day), block, flexible. | Periodized > non-periodized by a small margin. Undulating > linear only in trained people. |
| **Volume / frequency / intensity** | Hard sets per week, sessions per week per movement, load as % of 1RM. | Strong meta-analytic dose-response data, mostly for large muscles. |
| **Specificity and transfer** | You get strongest in the exact movement you train. Related movements improve less. | Consistent across trials ([Mang et al. 2022](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/3c0357cb66b54c6b9a598b0ca578600a/scj-transfer-between-lifts-2022.pdf)). |
| **Fatigue, deload, fitness-fatigue model** | Training builds fitness and fatigue. A deload cuts load to let fatigue clear. Banister's model formalizes the idea. | Deloads rest on expert consensus plus one RCT. The Banister model is fragile for individuals. |
| **Typical error (TE), smallest worthwhile change (SWC)** | Statistics for deciding whether an individual's change is real or noise. | Standard sports-science method ([Hopkins 2004](https://www.sportsci.org/jour/04/wghtests.htm)), barely used in gym apps. |

The second map sorts claims Javier will meet in books, forums and apps by how much weight they can bear. "Evidence-based" here means at least one meta-analysis or a consistent body of trials. "Thin evidence" means one or two small studies. "Convention" means rules coaches use that no study has tested. "Folklore" means claims whose evidence is absent or that the evidence contradicts.

| Tier | Claims |
|---|---|
| **Evidence-based** | Autoregulation ≥ fixed % loading. Strength dose-response flattens early. Frequency helps strength modestly. Heavy loads beat light ones for 1RM, and moderate loads come close. Proximity to failure barely matters for strength. RIR misjudged by ~1 rep, worse beyond 12 reps. 1RM test noise ~4%. Rest >2 min for heavy sets. Supersets don't hurt long-term gains. Whatever is done first in a session gains most. Concurrent endurance work doesn't blunt maximal strength. Strength holds through ~3–4 weeks off. |
| **Thin evidence** | Rep progression vs load progression (one RCT). Deload by full cessation slightly hurts strength (one RCT). Neck, external-rotator and adductor trainability (small trials). One session a week maintains strength (one large RCT plus a review). |
| **Convention** | Increment sizes. Rep-range widths. "3 failed sessions → −10%". Deload = ~50% of sets for ~1 week. RP's MEV/MAV/MRV volume landmarks. SBS training-max adjustments. Counting synergist sets at exactly 0.5. Gap-based load reductions after time off. |
| **Folklore or contradicted** | Poliquin structural-balance ratios as targets. H:Q ratio as a hamstring-injury predictor. Deloads "resensitizing" muscle. Hold time as a strength measure. "AI-built" programs in commercial apps (no published model). |

Two cross-cutting caveats shape everything below. First, almost all the trials last 5–16 weeks, involve mostly young men, and test large barbell or machine lifts. A one-year program across neck, wrists and toes runs far outside the sampled space. Second, **nothing in the literature tests a program like this one**: irregular 1–6 day weeks, ~100 movements, concurrent martial arts. Every recommendation for Javier is therefore a reasoned extrapolation, and the report labels it that way.

## Strength needs few sets per movement, but every movement needs its own

### The dose-response curve flattens after about four weekly sets

The largest dose-response analysis is Pelland et al.: 67 studies, 2,058 participants, published in *Sports Medicine* in 2026. Strength rises with weekly volume, with diminishing returns "more pronounced for strength" than for muscle growth ([SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)). The preprint quantifies it. Each extra weekly set adds about **0.21% strength**. The **minimum effective dose is 1 set a week**, 2 sets is "higher efficiency", 3–4 is "intermediate", and from about 5 sets the extra gain no longer reliably exceeds the smallest detectable effect of ~4% ([Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)). Per session, the same group puts the point of no detectable extra strength at about **2 direct sets** ([SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)). Two caveats matter. The strength studies averaged only ~8 sets a week, so high volumes are barely sampled. And a powerlifting study cited in the preprint found 1–3 sets insufficient where 3–9 sufficed, so trained lifters probably need more than the pooled minimum. Researchers studying competitive powerlifters arrived at a similar "minimum effective training dose" from another direction: **3–6 working sets a week of 1–5 reps per lift, at >80% 1RM and RPE 7.5–9.5, over 1–3 sessions** ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)).

Read together, these give a practical ladder for a returning intermediate. About 1–2 hard sets a week maintains a quality and still gives small gains in a neglected one. About 3–6 is the efficient zone for continued gain on a main lift. Beyond 8–10 the return per set is small. That ladder is an inference across studies, not one paper's prescription. Its implication for Javier is favourable: **breadth is cheap per item**. Most of the ~100 movements can make measurable progress on one or two hard sets per exposure.

### Frequency helps strength, and specificity means indirect work doesn't count

Pelland's model finds a positive frequency effect for strength but not for hypertrophy. It predicts **~12.7% gains at one session a week versus ~17.3% at two**, with sharply diminishing returns beyond that ([Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)). Older volume-equated analyses found no frequency effect ([Ralston et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)), so part of the "frequency benefit" is extra volume and extra practice of the tested movement. Either way, **two exposures a week for the main lifts and at least one for everything else** is the evidence-shaped target.

Specificity is what makes the "no weak links" goal expensive. Strength gains are largest in the trained exercise. Related lifts improve partially and asymmetrically: squats transfer to leg press more than the reverse. Multi-joint training raises untrained single-joint lifts less than direct single-joint training does ([Mang et al. 2022](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/3c0357cb66b54c6b9a598b0ca578600a/scj-transfer-between-lifts-2022.pdf)). In Pelland's per-session model, **direct** set counting predicted strength best, while counting synergist sets at 0.5 ("fractional" counting) predicted hypertrophy best ([SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)). The design rule follows. Count compound sets at 0.5 toward synergist *muscles* if tracking muscle volume, but credit *movement* strength only from direct sets of that movement or a near-identical variant. Neck, toes, wrist deviation and hip rotation get essentially zero indirect loading, so they need their own sets.

### Heavy loads matter for 1RM; failure doesn't

Across 28 studies, heavy loads (≤8RM) beat light loads (>15RM) for strength (SMD ~0.6). The gap between heavy and moderate loads (9–15RM) was **small and not significant (SMD ~0.27)**, and hypertrophy did not differ by load ([Lopez et al. 2021](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)). Main compounds therefore belong mostly in the 3–6 rep zone. Small-joint and isolation items lose little by living at 8–15 reps, which is also safer and easier to measure.

Proximity to failure is the cleanest "you don't need to" finding in the field. In a meta-regression of 55 studies, the strength slope per rep in reserve was essentially zero (β = 0.003, CI −0.012 to 0.018), while hypertrophy improved modestly closer to failure ([Robinson et al. 2024](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654)). Failure versus non-failure gives only a trivial hypertrophy edge (ES 0.19) ([Refalo et al. 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full)). Training to failure also slows recovery by 24–48 h ([Morán-Navarro et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)). For someone who teaches kung fu four nights a week, **1–3 RIR on compounds costs no strength and saves fatigue**.

### Session design: what goes first gains most

A meta-analysis of exercise order found that **whatever is trained first gains more strength**: multi-joint first gave ES 0.32, single-joint first ES 0.58 ([Nunes et al. 2021](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059795?lng=en)). Supersets cut session time with **no chronic strength or hypertrophy cost (strength SMD 0.10, ns)**. Agonist–antagonist pairs even raised reps completed, while pairing two exercises for the same muscles cut volume load ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)). Trained lifters need **>2 min rest** to maximize strength on heavy sets, and 60–120 s suffices elsewhere ([Grgic et al. 2018](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4047011)). Full-body and split routines produced near-identical 1RM gains when volume was equated (bench +17.5% vs +18.1%) ([Evangelista et al. 2021](https://www.scielo.br/j/eins/a/sQYb5T8zwYNzgNCpK8pDYwF/?lang=en)). A full-body rotating queue is therefore evidence-compatible. Within it, the main lifts go first with long rest, then supersets or tri-sets of non-competing small items such as neck + calf + wrist, or external rotation + hip rotation. The first slot should rotate, and **known weak links should move to the front**.

### Arithmetic, not physiology, caps the hundred-movement program

The time budget is where the plan meets reality. A hard set plus rest costs ~2.5–3.5 min done straight, or ~1.5–2 min effective inside supersets. A 60–75 min session therefore holds ~20–30 working sets, and a 35-min session ~10–14. Weekly coverage of everything looks like this: ~15–20 main compounds at 3–6 sets (60–100 sets), plus ~80 small items at 1 set (~80 sets), for **~140–180 hard sets a week**. Three sessions of ~25 sets give ~75. (This budget arithmetic is the researchers' estimate, not a published figure.) An earlier ~130-sets/week draft fits only on 4–5 training days. On 1–2-day weeks the queue must protect the main lifts and let small items slide to a **2–3 week rotation**. Exposure frequencies below once a week are **not studied**. The closest evidence is maintenance data: one session a week at 1/3 or even 1/9 of the volume held strength for 32 weeks in young adults, provided load stayed heavy ([Bickel et al. 2011](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Exercise%20Dosing%20to%20Retain%20Resistance%20Training%20Adaptations%20in%20Young%20and%20Older%20Adults.pdf)). A review of maintenance dosing says the same: intensity is the variable to protect ([Spiering et al. 2021, summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/que-dosis-minima-se-necesita-para-mantener-la-fuerza-y-la-resistencia-aerobica-a-traves-del-tiempo/)).

### Kung fu: maximal strength is safe if heavy legs stay away from class

A 43-study meta-analysis of concurrent endurance and strength training found **no interference with maximal strength (SMD −0.06) or hypertrophy**. It did find a loss of explosive strength (SMD −0.28), and only when both were done in the same session; sessions **≥3 h apart showed no effect** ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)). No study covers martial arts specifically, so the following is extrapolation. Lift on non-kung-fu days or at least ~3 h before class. Keep heavy or high-volume lower-body work away from kicking and sparring nights, both for skill quality and for injury risk. Count kung fu nights as fatigue when deciding whether to take the lighter session in the queue.

## Performance-driven progression wins, but its numbers are convention

### Every progression model works; autoregulation has a small, fragile edge

Three meta-analyses compare autoregulated loading with fixed %1RM programs, and they agree on direction but not on size. Zhang et al. pooled 8 small studies of athletes and found SMD 0.64 favouring autoregulation (squat +4.6 kg, bench +3.2 kg). APRE drove the effect (SMD 0.78), RPE alone was not significant (0.17, only 15 people) and VBT was not significant either. Five of the eight studies used *estimated* rather than tested 1RM ([Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)). Hickmott et al. excluded APRE and found **no significant difference: +2.07 kg, p = 0.09** ([Hickmott et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)). A 2025 network meta-analysis ranks APRE > VBT > RPE > percentage ([JESF 2025 record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4094556)), but APRE's top rank rests partly on a non-randomized trial with methodological weaknesses ([Larsen et al. 2021](https://peerj.com/articles/10663)). The best single RCT, Helms 2018, found RPE loading equal to or slightly better than percentage loading in trained men (total +27.8 vs +23.6 kg, ns) ([Helms et al. 2018](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text)). **The honest summary is "small advantage at best, equivalence at worst."** The practical reason to autoregulate is fit, not effect size. Rules keyed to performance survive irregular schedules, and calendar-based percentage plans assume regular weekly exposures.

Double progression has **never been tested head-to-head** against these models. The nearest evidence is Plotkin et al. 2022. Adding load to stay within 8–12 reps and adding reps at a fixed load gave near-identical hypertrophy, with a ~2 kg squat-1RM edge for adding load (90% CI −2.4 to 7.8) ([Plotkin et al. 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142)). Mechanically, double progression *is* a rep-based autoregulation rule, and APRE is double progression with a set taken to failure and a lookup table for jump size. Javier's choice sits inside the evidence-supported family.

### Commercial apps converge on step-aware double progression

None of the major apps publishes its math, but their documented behaviour reduces to a few rule shapes. RP raises weight "by a few percentage points each week" and adds reps when the next dumbbell jump is too big. It adds or removes sets from pump, soreness and workload ratings, with no published thresholds ([RP help](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32600173777815-How-does-the-app-determine-when-to-add-weight-reps-and-sets)). MacroFactor (built by the Stronger by Science team) lowers future loads when a set comes in harder than the target RIR. Its "Expand Rep Range" option suggests 100×9 rather than 105×8 when the plate step is too large. Programs follow a sequence, not a calendar: "If you miss workouts… your program does not change" ([MacroFactor 373](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/373-why-does-the-app-sometimes-recommend-lowering-weight-or-reps); [MacroFactor 305](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/305-understanding-and-using-smart-progressions); [MacroFactor 382](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/382-what-happens-to-my-program-if-i-miss-workouts-or-take-time-off)). Alpha Progression adds reps before a big relative jump (7.5 → 10 kg is +33%) and lets recommendations go down ([Alpha Progression](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/alpha-progression-guide)). The SBS spreadsheets, per a secondary summary that needs checking against the originals, nudge a training max by about **+0.5% per rep beyond target and −1% per rep missed when 2 or more reps short** ([Lift Vault](https://liftvault.com/programs/strength/stronger-by-science-sbs-program-bundle-by-greg-nuckols/)). Liftosaur is the fully transparent reference. Its scripting language exposes `dp(increase, minReps, maxReps)`, Epley `calculate1RM`, RPE multiplier tables and plate-aware rounding as primitives ([Liftoscript docs](https://www.liftosaur.com/doc/liftoscript)). The "machine learning" in Fitbod and Juggernaut AI has no published model and offers nothing reproducible ([Fitbod blog](https://fitbod.me/blog/fitbod-algorithm); [JTS help](https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/3-how-juggernautai-is-individualized-to-you)). The common core worth copying: **double progression whose increments come from the equipment actually available, which adds reps when the next step overshoots and steps down when a set is harder than its RIR target.**

### RIR is accurate to about a rep, but only near failure and under 12 reps

An exploratory meta-analysis of 12 studies (414 people) found that lifters **underpredict reps to failure by ~0.95 reps**, with a between-person SD of 1.45 reps. Error grows sharply in sets with more than 12 reps to failure: the slope rises from 0.06 to **0.47 extra reps of error per rep**. **Training experience made no difference** ([Halperin et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)). Single studies agree. Errors were ~1.1–1.2 reps when predicting 4–5 RIR versus ~0.5–0.7 at 1 RIR, and accuracy did not improve over six weeks of practice ([SBS summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)). Coaches watching video are no better: their error is 4.8 reps a third of the way into a set and 1.2 reps near the end ([Emanuel et al. 2022](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/165)). Practitioners therefore suggest assuming you leave ~1 more rep than you think, rating RIR at the end of the set, and occasionally taking a final set to failure after predicting RIR to calibrate each exercise ([SBS summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)). One finding is directly useful for software: individual effort profiles predict about twice as well as pooled ones ([Jukic et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10901726)). A per-exercise calibration offset learned from Javier's own failure sets beats any generic RPE chart.

### e1RM formulas are fine at low reps and unreliable at high reps

Reynolds et al. predicted 1RM from 5RM, 10RM and 20RM tests. Leg-press error grew from 16 kg to 29 kg SEE, and chest-press error from 3.0 to 7.4 kg, so the authors recommend **no more than 10 reps** in linear equations ([Reynolds et al. 2006](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)). No equation is best for every lift ([Corvos Hidalgo et al. 2022](https://efyc.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/article/view/efyce222)). A 2026 preprint on 303,494 near-failure sets from Fitbod users notes that Epley grows linearly without bound at high reps. It proposes a weight-dependent formula that cut inconsistency by 17–22%, but it was optimized for within-user consistency rather than against tested 1RMs ([Marzagao 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495)). For Javier, the choice of formula matters less than these rules: **compute e1RM only from sets with reps + RIR ≤ ~10–12, and keep each movement's rep range stable**. A consistent bias then cancels out of a self-referenced trend. Plugging reps + RIR into Epley is the usual practitioner adjustment and the one Liftosaur and the research notes use. No study has validated RIR-adjusted e1RM against tested 1RM.

### Increments and rep-range width: one equation replaces guesswork

No peer-reviewed study tests increment size, microloading or rep-range width. The best available rule is arithmetic on the e1RM curve. At constant strength, Epley implies that a relative load step of Δw/w costs about **Δr ≈ (Δw/w) × (30 + r) reps**. A 2.5 kg jump on an 80 kg squat at 6 reps costs ~1 rep, so a 4–6 range is enough. A 1 kg step on an 8 kg wrist curl at 15 reps costs ~5–6 reps. A 2.5 kg step on a 10 kg dumbbell at 20 reps costs ~12 reps, which needs a range such as 10–25. (These are derived numbers, not tested ones.) **Javier's light frame (~60 kg bodyweight) makes this bite harder than usual.** His absolute loads are lower, so standard 2.5 kg steps are proportionally larger, and microplates or rep-range widening matter more. Rippetoe's convention, "microload when you start wondering whether you should", says the same thing in gym language ([Starting Strength forum](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html)).

### "3 stalls then −10%" is novice-program tradition

The stall-and-reset rule in openGym and StrongLifts traces to Rippetoe's *Practical Programming*, as paraphrased on its forum. Retry a missed weight 2–3 times, then "deload 10% (not 20)", never dropping below 90% of prior 5RMs. If stalls recur, change programming rather than keep resetting ([Starting Strength forum](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html)). No study validates the threshold or the size of the drop. It is a reasonable default with two known failure modes. For small items, 10% can be less than one plate step. For a returning lifter, early "stalls" are often mis-set starting loads or kung fu fatigue rather than true plateaus. RIR logging gives a better test: a session with the same reps but a higher RIR is progress, not a stall.

### Deloads: experts endorse fatigue-triggered, volume-first deloads

Deloading rests mainly on a Delphi consensus of 21–34 strength and physique coaches. They agreed (100%) that deloads can be pre-planned *or* autoregulated, and should happen "when athletes feel fatigued, regardless of mesocycle week". Sets, reps and training days should be cut (100%), possibly to a minimum effective dose, while intensity may stay high. The panel reached **no consensus on deload length or frequency**; the familiar "~1 week every 4–6 weeks" comes from coaching practice ([Bell et al. 2023](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf)). The only RCT tested a full week off in the middle of a 9-week program. It produced **no extra muscle growth and slightly worse strength** (isometric knee extension favoured continuous training, posterior probability 0.92) ([Coleman et al. 2024](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)). Javier's fatigue-triggered approach therefore matches the consensus, provided deloads cut *volume* rather than stopping training. His irregular schedule also creates natural deloads: a one-session week already is one.

### Periodization models matter less than turning up

Periodized programs beat non-periodized ones for 1RM by ES ~0.2–0.4, shrinking to 0.23 once outliers are removed, with signs of publication bias ([Williams et al. 2017](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf)). A 35-RCT meta-analysis reports undulating > linear for strength (ES ~0.31), an effect seen in trained subjects (~0.61) and nearly absent in untrained ones. Those numbers come from secondary summaries because the primary abstract could not be reached ([Sci-Sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/effects-intensity-volume-periodization-strength-hypertrophy-238/); [Examine](https://examine.com/faq/are-periodized-training-programs-superior-for-strength-gains)). The edge is real but small next to the cost of irregular attendance. The cheapest way to capture it in a queue is to give each main compound **two variants with different rep zones** (e.g. heavy 3–5 and moderate 6–8), each with its own double progression. Missed days then never "break" the plan.

### Detraining is slow, regain is fast, and the Banister model is not worth building

Maximal strength is largely unaffected for **~4 weeks off** (g < 0.2), with losses accelerating after that ([Bosquet 2013, via SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)). Retraining recovers lost strength quickly: after a 3-week break, untrained men ended level with continuous trainers ([Ogasawara, CoachSci](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol171/ogasawar.htm)). Nuckols' heuristics are that regain takes about half the break (range 1/3–2/3). After a break of under a month, cut loads ~20% in week one. After a longer break, start week one at ~1/3 of prior loads and close the gap linearly ([SBS detraining](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)). These are coaching heuristics, not trial results, but they set expectations: **Javier's first months back are a regain phase that will look like rapid progress**, and any baseline set inside it is inflated.

The fitness-fatigue (Banister) model is the field's classic formal model: performance = fitness − fatigue, each decaying exponentially. For resistance training it is fragile. In 2.1 million simulations, predictions became unsatisfactory once measurement CV exceeded ~4%, the two gain parameters correlated at r = 0.88–0.99 (a sign of overfitting), and sparse testing worsened predictions ([Stephens Hemingway et al. 2020](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/691845)). Most published fits were never tested out of sample ([Swinton et al. 2021](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1871154)). Training-log e1RM, with RIR error of about ±1 rep (~±3%), sits at that noise edge, and an irregular schedule makes exposures sparse. **At most, show two moving averages as an informational "form" display. Never let it prescribe loads.** Readiness questionnaires and HRV are similarly weak for strength-specific decisions. Performance-based markers (RIR-rated sets, bar speed, e1RM trend) have the best support ([Helms et al. 2020](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/b67c0527-5e33-45d9-886c-746d3a659ea0/full)).

## Small joints respond to load, but the evidence thins at the edges

The "no weak links" ambition runs into the thinnest part of the literature. The pattern is consistent: **actions with a sizeable muscle and an external load that can be applied and measured respond to progressive overload like any lift. Actions that are mainly positional or coordinative have almost no strength-training evidence in healthy adults.**

The neck is trainable, with modest evidence. A systematic review found only 3 eligible studies, all in male rugby or American football players and all of "neutral" quality. The clearest protocol (Geary 2014) used **3 × 10 s harness isometrics in each of four directions, twice a week for 5 weeks**, and raised isometric strength **~17–21%**. A helmet-mounted rotating weight (6 × 50 revolutions each way, twice a week, 7 weeks) produced one of the few data points on **axial rotation**. No adverse events were reported, and **no evidence shows that neck strengthening prevents concussion** ([Daly et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)). A broader review notes the smallest gains came from low-intensity or low-frequency programmes, and that the link between greater strength and head stabilization is limited ([Sports Med 2016 review](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4040015?lng=en)). Retraction (chin tuck) is essentially unstudied as a strength target.

For the shoulder, eccentric external-rotation training (2×/week, 6 weeks) raised eccentric ER strength **+24%**, but cut active range of motion by 7% ([Vetter et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0293439)). The title of a 2021 youth handball RCT reports "No Added Benefit of 8 Weeks of Shoulder External Rotation Strength Training… Over Usual Handball Training Alone"; its numbers were not retrievable ([JOSPT 2021](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2021.9957)). For kung fu, the analogue is that the sport itself may already train some of these qualities. Scapular tilt and downward rotation appear in the literature almost only as EMG or motor-control topics, not as strength outcomes.

The hip adductors are the best-evidenced "unusual" target. An 8-week Copenhagen adduction programme raised eccentric adduction strength **+35.7%** and abduction +20.3% in young footballers, with median soreness of 0–2/10 and 91% compliance ([Ishøi et al. 2016](https://www.ucviden.dk/en/publications/large-eccentric-strength-increase-using-the-copenhagen-adduction-/)). The authors argue that **reps or time under tension are the practical progression** for this bodyweight exercise. They also cite a 41% reduction in groin problems with an adductor programme ([BJSM editorial](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/19/1066)). Hip internal and external rotation can be loaded with cables and ankle straps in both hip-flexed and hip-extended positions, but no verified trial uses them as a primary outcome.

For the foot, a systematic review of 11 studies found intrinsic-muscle (short-foot) training gives low-certainty gains in gait function, very-low-certainty gains in balance, and **no effect on arch kinematics** ([Willemse et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13047-021-00509-0)). Inversion and eversion are standard ankle-rehab strength targets. Tibial internal-rotator strength is measured in post-ACL rehab ([PMC3560515](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3560515/)), but there is no healthy-population training evidence. Toe abduction is effectively a motor skill. Grip and wrist flexion and extension are clearly trainable. Finger extension, thumb and finger ab/adduction have no verified training trials.

The split that follows for Javier's list is practical. Neck flexion, extension and lateral flexion; shoulder ER/IR; protraction, retraction, elevation and depression; hip ab/adduction and rotation; plantar flexion and dorsiflexion; inversion and eversion; grip and wrist flexion/extension and pronation/supination can all take the same double-progression rule and an e1RM-style number. Scapular tilt, toe abduction, the foot arch and tibial rotation should be logged as **skill items with a quality or hold score, kept out of the strength weak-link ranking**. Neck work should start with submaximal isometrics before dynamic loading and progress slowly, a precaution inferred from the trial protocols rather than tested.

Cheap measurement is possible for most edge movements. A €10–20 digital crane or luggage scale on a strap, anchored to a rack or cable column, measures isometric pulls for neck (with a harness), shoulder rotation, hip rotation, ab/adduction, ankle movements and finger extension. A bathroom scale measures toe flexion, and a hand dynamometer measures grip. The catch is reliability. In the closest validated analogue, a load-cell neck test, reliability within a session was good (CV 6–9%) but between sessions it fell to **CV 14.5–19.8%** ([PMC9031103](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9031103)). No verified reliability data exist for DIY crane-scale setups. Fixed joint angle, anchor point and lever arm, plus best-of-3 attempts, are the controllable parts. One trap: **hold time is an endurance measure, not a force measure**, because a 20% longer hold is not 20% more strength. Progress isometric holds by load at a fixed time, or test them with a scale.

## Noise, not formulas, decides what counts as a weak link

### A single comparison can't see changes smaller than about 8%

A systematic review of 32 studies found 1RM tests highly reliable for groups (median ICC 0.97), with a **median CV of 4.2%**: 3.8% with familiarization, 5.3% without, 3.3% in people with training experience. Retests also showed a learning effect, coming in a median **5.5 kg higher for lower-body lifts and 1.8 kg for upper-body lifts** ([Grgic et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z)). Hopkins' standard method for individuals treats a change as probably real when it exceeds about **2 × the typical error** if no smallest worthwhile change is defined. He also warns that "most performance tests are noisier than the smallest worthwhile change" ([Hopkins 2017](https://sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm); [Hopkins 2004](https://www.sportsci.org/jour/04/wghtests.htm)). With a 4% CV, one session against one session needs ~8% change before it is likely real. That is more than a trained lifter gains in a month or two. Averaging n sessions divides the error by √n ([Hopkins 2017](https://sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm)). Comparing the median of 4 sessions at each end therefore brings the threshold down to roughly **4% for barbell e1RM and ~15% for a DIY isometric with ~15% CV**. (That is arithmetic on Hopkins' rule, not a published threshold.) "Every movement measurably stronger by summer 2027" is thus achievable for loaded lifts. For improvised isometric tests it is achievable only if gains are large, which the neck (~17–21% in 5 weeks) and adductor (~36% in 8 weeks) trials suggest is plausible early on.

No published smallest-detectable-change figure exists for e1RM taken from everyday training sets. Javier will have to estimate his own. Hopkins' method does this from the residual scatter around a trend line once there are **at least 10 data points** ([Hopkins 2017](https://sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm)).

### Trend slopes beat baselines, and need 8–12 exposures

Hopkins' monitoring method replaces a single baseline with a **linear trend over a chosen window**, compared against a "target trend" (desired change ÷ period). Points or slopes are flagged as "likely" (>75%) or "very likely" (>90%) substantial ([Hopkins 2017](https://sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm)). A rough calculation in the research notes shows why patience is mandatory. With 4% session-to-session noise, the slope's 95% CI over 8 weekly points is about ±1.2% per week, the same size as a good intermediate's weekly gain. **A stall can only be told apart from ~1%/week progress after ~8–12 exposures**, which at 1–2 exposures a week means a 6–12-week window. A rolling *maximum* of e1RM, the "best set" Javier planned to use, is biased upward by noise, only ratchets up, and cannot show decline. A rolling median or a robust regression slope (Theil–Sen) is unbiased and gives a confidence interval. Statistical-process-control charts (CUSUM, EWMA) would catch small sustained drifts sooner. That is an extrapolation, since none has been validated on strength logs.

With ~100 movements, multiple comparisons become a design problem. If each movement is flagged at a "likely" threshold, a sizeable handful will look weak by chance at any moment. Hopkins explicitly advises simulating false-flag rates before trusting flags. **Requiring a weak-link flag to persist across two consecutive evaluations** is a cheap guard; this is a design inference, not a published rule.

For movements trained above ~12 reps, conversion to 1RM breaks down: formula bias grows, and RIR error adds about half a rep per rep beyond 12. Those movements should be compared **only with their own history in a fixed rep bracket**. The Epley-style number can still serve as a convenient *index*, valid within that bracket, but it is not a real 1RM and cannot be compared with low-rep lifts. Ranking very different exercises against each other is done by expressing each one's change in **its own noise units** (percent change ÷ typical error), not in kilograms or bodyweight multiples. That puts a noisy neck test and a stable deadlift on one scale. No study has compared this against other ranking methods.

### Reference ratios: keep left-to-right, demote the rest

The evidence for strength ratios is far weaker than their popularity suggests. A meta-analysis of 78 studies (8,319 hamstring injuries) found **isokinetic testing, where H:Q norms come from, unrelated to hamstring injury in all 11 meta-analyses**. None of 13 strength variables was significantly associated ([Green et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/18/1081)). The shoulder offers one positive signal: in professional pitchers, preseason ER weakness and the prone ER:IR ratio were associated with shoulder injury ([Byram et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0363546509360404)). That is one correlational cohort in throwers, not a target for a kung fu practitioner. Neck flexion:extension runs ~0.70–0.82 in rugby players, but there is "no agreement on… requisite strength levels". The ratio's between-session CV of **~21–24%** is far noisier than either component ([PMC9031103](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9031103)). Poliquin-style structural-balance ratios (front squat 85% of back squat, close-grip bench 90% of bench, preacher curl 40% of bench) are presented as adapted from Poliquin's material and Russian weightlifting literature, **with no studies cited**, and no peer-reviewed validation was found ([Thibaudeau](https://thibarmy.com/know-your-ratios-destroy-weaknesses/)). Gym-machine ratios also aren't isokinetic torque ratios: cable cams, lever arms and stabilizer demands make the numbers incomparable to clinical norms.

Left:right comparison on the same unilateral exercise is the exception, because same-day, same-movement noise largely cancels. The conventional flag is a limb symmetry index below 90%. With ~4% noise per side, a single-session ratio can wobble ~±6% by chance, so the deficit must persist. One known trap: LSI looks fine when *both* sides weaken. ACL research recommends comparing against estimated prior capacity instead ([Wellsandt et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28355978/)). For Javier, that means pairing L:R with each side's own trend.

### Three alternatives to the return-ramp baseline

A fixed baseline set at the end of a ramp has three problems. Its timing is arbitrary. It sits inside a regain phase that inflates later "growth" unevenly: lifts trained before the break regain fast, new small-joint items show novice gains. And first exposures carry a learning effect ([Grgic et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z)). Single baseline measures also lose predictive validity as strength changes over time ([Green et al. 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/18/1081)). The research suggests three simpler options. The best is a **rolling-window slope** over the last 8–12 exposures compared against a target trend, which needs no baseline event: the ramp simply becomes the steep early part of the trend. Second is **percent of own best e1RM** over the last 12 months, an "estimated prior capacity" view useful after layoffs. Third is an **automatic first stable window**: the median of exposures 3–5 of each movement, discarding the first two. No study has compared these.

```text
PER-SET METRIC
  eligible  = (reps + RIR <= 12) and (RIR <= 3)
  e1RM      = load_total * (1 + (reps + RIR + rir_bias[ex]) / 30)    # Epley on reps-to-failure
              load_total includes bodyweight for dips, pull-ups and similar
              rir_bias[ex] starts at +1 (people underpredict by ~1 rep), updated by calibration sets
  non-eligible sets -> same formula, but tagged with a rep bracket (12-20, 20-30) and
              compared only with sets in the same bracket

PROGRESSION (evaluated once per exposure, on the work sets; ranges fixed per movement)
  ranges    compounds 3-6 (or heavy/moderate variants 3-5 and 6-8), mid isolation 6-12,
            small joints 8-15 where steps <= ~10% of load, else 10-25
  width     top - bottom >= (step / load) * (30 + top)               # Epley arithmetic
  if all work sets reach top at RIR >= 1:     load += 1 step
     (optional) last set exceeds top by >= 3 at RIR >= 1: load += 2 steps   # APRE-like
  elif top reached only at RIR 0:             hold load
  elif within range:                          hold load, aim +1 rep
  elif below bottom:                          miss += 1   (not counted if gap > 14 d or fatigue flag)
  if miss == 3 at same load and no rep or RIR improvement:
      compounds: load *= 0.9 (openGym default, convention; floor 90% of previous best)
      small items: load -= 1 step, or reset reps to bottom of range
  step      = smallest available plate, stack or dumbbell change; if step/load > ~5-10%, widen the range

GAPS (per movement, since last exposure)
  <= 14 d normal | 15-28 d repeat last load | 4-8 wk -10 to -20% | longer: short ramp

GLOBAL DELOAD (reactive)
  trigger   >= 2 main lifts in a miss streak, or main-lift e1RM > 5-7% below the best of its
            last 3 exposures on two consecutive sessions
  content   next 2-4 sessions (~1 week): ~50% of sets, same load or -10%, RIR >= 3-4
  skip      if the past 7 days held <= 1 session

SCHEDULING (due-score queue)
  due       = days_since_last / target_interval   (main lifts 3-4 d, others 7 d, max tolerated 14 d)
  priority  = due * (1.5 if weak-link flag else 1)
  session   main lifts first (rest >= 2 min); then supersets or tri-sets of non-competing items
            (60-90 s); rotate the first slot; weak links early
  kung fu   heavy or high-volume lower body only on non-class days or >= 3 h before class

WEAK-LINK ANALYSIS (outside the app, every ~4 weeks)
  per movement, after >= 8 exposures:
    start     = median metric of exposures 3-5
    slope     = Theil-Sen over the last 8-12 exposures, in % per month
    TE        = default (4% e1RM, ~15% DIY isometric) -> own residual SD once n >= 10
    z_growth  = (median of last 4 exposures - start) / start / (TE / sqrt(4))
  flag if slope is likely below the target trend, or z_growth is in the bottom decile,
          or L:R < 0.90 over >= 3 exposures; and the flag persists across 2 evaluations
  response  +1 set per exposure, shorter target interval, earlier slot, for the next 6-8 weeks
```

Two of these parameters deserve a word of honesty. The ×0.9 reset, the deload content and the gap thresholds come from coaching practice ([Starting Strength forum](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html); [Bell et al. 2023](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf); [SBS detraining](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)), not from trials. The weak-link statistics borrow Hopkins' framework, but the exact windows and the decile cut-off are design choices that Javier should check by simulation on his own data before trusting them.

## Conclusion

The surprising result is not which algorithm wins. It is that **the algorithms barely differ, while measurement noise differs by a factor of four between movements**. Javier's real engineering problem is therefore not "which progression model" but "how to decide, per movement, whether a change is real". That reframes the app. The progression engine can stay simple and conventional, and the novel, valuable part is the analysis layer: per-movement typical error, rolling robust slopes, noise-scaled ranking and persistence-filtered flags. That layer is exactly what no commercial app documents and what a software engineer can build better than a coach.

The second implication is about scope. The evidence makes each movement cheap (1–2 hard sets per exposure suffice to register gains) but makes coverage expensive, because strength does not transfer fully between movements. "No weak links" across ~100 movements in one year is plausible for every loadable action if the queue protects weekly exposure. It turns into a scheduling problem, not a physiology problem, whenever kung fu and life shrink the week to one or two sessions. Deciding in advance which items may slide to a 2–3 week rotation, rather than letting the queue decide by accident, is the single choice most likely to determine whether the summer-2027 audit shows a hundred green numbers or a long tail of "unclear".
