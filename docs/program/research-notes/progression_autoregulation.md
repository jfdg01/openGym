# Load progression and autoregulation algorithms for resistance training

Evidence labels used throughout: **[MA]** = meta-analysis / systematic review; **[RCT]** = single randomized (or controlled) trial; **[OBS]** = single non-randomized / observational / validation study; **[PRE]** = preprint, not peer reviewed; **[EXP]** = expert / coaching opinion or convention; **[BK]** = from researcher's background knowledge, NOT verified against a source in this session (report writer should treat as unverified or drop).

## 1. Which progression models exist and how do they compare in studies?

### Takeaway
Every model studied (fixed %1RM, RPE/RIR load selection, APRE, flexible periodization, VBT) produces strength gains in trained people; meta-analyses disagree on whether autoregulation beats fixed loading. The most careful one (Hickmott 2022) finds no significant difference for load autoregulation (MD ≈ +2 kg, p = 0.09); Zhang 2021 and a 2025 network meta-analysis favour autoregulation, mainly APRE, but these rest on few, small, partly non-randomized studies, so the honest summary is "small advantage at best, equivalence at worst."

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses / systematic reviews**
- [MA] Zhang et al. 2021, *Frontiers in Physiology* 12:651112: 8 studies (17 reports), 166 athletes (151 M), 5–10 week interventions; methods APRE (3 studies), RPE (2), VBT (3). Overall autoregulation vs fixed loading for 1RM: SMD 0.64 (95% CI 0.43–0.85), I² = 0%. Squat MD 4.64 kg (0.56–8.73); bench MD 3.21 kg (0.34–6.09). By method: APRE SMD 0.78 (0.54–1.02); RPE 0.17 (−0.33 to 0.67, ns); VBT 0.43 (−0.24 to 1.10, ns); between-method difference p = 0.08. Shorter programs (≤~7–8 wk) SMD 0.87 vs longer (8–10 wk) 0.32 (p = 0.05); note the paper's Results section contradicts its abstract about which label goes with which value. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- [MA] Zhang 2021 limitations: only experienced athletes; 5 of 8 studies (117 subjects) used *estimated* 1RM rather than tested; no blinding; only 4 RCTs (2 matched-pair, 2 non-randomized); PEDro 3 good / 5 fair; RPE subgroup n = 15 and VBT n = 20 vs APRE n = 53. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- [MA] Hickmott et al. 2022, *Sports Medicine – Open* (s40798-021-00404-9): 18 studies in review, 15 meta-analysed (6 load, 9 volume; 133 load participants, 308 volume). Load autoregulation (RIR-based RPE and VBT) vs standardized %1RM: 1RM MD = 2.07 kg (95% CI −0.32 to 4.46), p = 0.09, SMD 0.21 → "autoregulated and standardized load prescription produced similar improvements in strength." APRE not included. — [Hickmott et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)
- [MA] Hickmott 2022, volume autoregulation (velocity-loss thresholds, sets and intensity equated): ≤25% velocity loss better for 1RM (MD 2.32 kg, 0.33–4.31, SMD 0.23); >25% better for hypertrophy (MD 0.61 cm², SMD 0.28). No study had tested an RPE-based "stop the set/session" volume rule chronically. — [Hickmott et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)
- [MA, systematic review without pooling] Larsen et al. 2021, *PeerJ* 9:e10663: 14 studies, 30 groups, 356 participants; methods RPE/RIR (3 studies), APRE (1), flexible nonlinear periodization (2), velocity targets (3), velocity loss (6). Conclusion: "both subjective and objective autoregulation methods are effective for enhancing maximal strength." RIR/RPE vs %: Graham & Cleather 2019 favoured RPE; Helms 2018 small non-significant difference. Flexible periodization inconsistent (Colquhoun 2017 no difference). Recommends RIR-based RPE for athletes without velocity devices "since it may offer small benefits over percent-based training"; APRE and flexible-periodization evidence to be interpreted cautiously; most evidence from trained males. — [Larsen et al. 2021](https://peerj.com/articles/10663)
- [MA, network] 2025 network meta-analysis, *Journal of Exercise Science & Fitness* 23(4):360–369, doi 10.1016/j.jesf.2025.07.006: SUCRA rankings back squat 1RM APRE 93.0%, RPE 66.8%, VBRT 27.0%, percentage-based 13.2% (no moderate/large between-intervention effects for squat); bench press APRE 97.1%, VBRT 57.1%, RPE 29.9%, percentage-based 15.9% (percentage-based vs APRE SMD −0.83, −1.22 to −0.44). Authors conclude APRE > VBRT > RPE > PBRT. Number of included studies not visible on the record page. — [JESF 2025 NMA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4094556)

**Primary method papers**
- [RCT] Helms et al. 2018, *Frontiers in Physiology* (fphys.2018.00247), "RPE vs. Percentage 1RM Loading in Periodized Programs Matched for Sets and Repetitions": 21 trained men (1RM group n = 11, RPE group n = 10), 8 weeks, squat + bench 3×/week daily undulating. 1RM gains: bench +9.64 ± 5.36 kg (%) vs +10.70 ± 3.30 (RPE); squat +13.91 ± 5.89 vs +17.05 ± 5.44; total +23.55 vs +27.75 kg. Between-group ns; magnitude-based inference: small RPE advantage likely for squat (ES 0.50, 79% chance) and total (ES 0.48, 72%). Muscle thickness similar. Conclusion: "Both loading-types are effective… RPE-based loading may provide a small 1RM strength advantage in a majority of individuals." RPE targets were 5–9. — [Helms et al. 2018 abstract](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text); [SBS summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- [OBS] Mann et al. 2010 APRE (college football players): largest bench effect in Larsen's review, ES 0.83, but "non-randomised and had methodological weaknesses." — [Larsen et al. 2021](https://peerj.com/articles/10663)
- [EXP] APRE 6 protocol as described by a practitioner: Set 1 = 50% of est. 6RM × 10; Set 2 = 75% × 6; Set 3 = 100% of 6RM to (technical) failure; Set 4 = load adjusted from set-3 reps, to max. Examples from the chart: 10 reps (8–12 band) → +5–10 lb; 5 reps → same weight; 3 reps → −5 lb. The set-4 result sets next week's load. — [Long Term Training (Substack)](https://longtermtraining.substack.com/p/apre-removing-the-guesswork-for-strength)
- [MA] Zhang 2021 describes APRE generically: first two sets pre-set, fourth-set load adjusted by third-set reps; up if target exceeded, down otherwise. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- [OBS] Zourdos et al. 2016 RIR-based RPE scale (RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR…; half-points allowed): in experienced and novice squatters RPE was strongly inversely related to bar velocity at all intensities; experienced lifters rated maximal lifts higher (closer to 10), suggesting better calibration. — [Stronger by Science, Androulakis-Korakakis 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- [OBS] Helms et al. 2017 (15 powerlifters): RPE vs %1RM correlations r = 0.88–0.91 across squat/bench/deadlift; RPE strongly inversely related to velocity. — [Stronger by Science 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- Velocity-based training: noted only. Velocity loss of ~10–30% appears favourable; 20% loss gave ES 1.53 in Pareja-Blanco 2017 [RCT]; velocity targets ≈ similar to or better than %-based. Requires a device. — [Larsen et al. 2021](https://peerj.com/articles/10663)

### Inferences
- The direction of evidence is consistent (autoregulation ≥ fixed %), but the size is small (≈2–5 kg over 6–10 weeks in trained lifters) and fragile. APRE's top ranking rests largely on Mann 2010 and a few small trials, so it is the least trustworthy "winner."
- Double progression has not been tested head-to-head against RPE/APRE in any study found. Mechanically it is a rep-based autoregulation rule: load rises only when performance shows it can. APRE is essentially double progression with a failure set and a lookup table for the size of the jump.
- For an irregular 1–6 days/week schedule, models keyed to sessions or performance (double progression, APRE, RIR-based) fit better than calendar-based %1RM periodization, which assumes regular weekly exposures. This is an inference, not tested.
- [BK] RTS/Tuchscherer-style RPE charts map (reps, RPE) to %e1RM, e.g. 1 rep @10 = 100%, 5 reps @8 ≈ 81%. Software can use such a table to turn any logged set into an e1RM, then prescribe next load = e1RM × chart%(target reps, target RPE). Exact table values were not verified in this session.

### Gaps
- Mann et al. 2010 primary paper (sample, duration, exact numbers) was not retrieved; PubMed/PMC blocked automated fetches.
- The full APRE 3/6/10 adjustment tables (upper vs lower body, kg values) were not verified from a primary source. [BK, unverified] Commonly reproduced APRE-6 table: set-3 reps 0–2 → −5 to −10 lb; 3–4 → 0 to −5 lb; 5–7 → same; 8–12 → +5 to +10 lb; ≥13 → +10 to +15 lb.
- No study found comparing double progression to any other model, nor any model in irregular-frequency trainees or concurrent martial-arts athletes.
- The 2025 NMA's study count and author list were not visible.

## 2. How accurate are e1RM formulas and self-reported RIR?

### Takeaway
Rep-to-1RM equations are reasonably accurate with low reps (roughly ≤10, best ≤5–7) and get worse as reps rise. No single equation is best for every lift. Self-reported RIR is off by about 1 rep on average (people tend to *underpredict* how many reps they have left), it is clearly better close to failure and in sets of ≤12 reps, and experience has little effect.

### Cited Findings
**e1RM formulas**
- [OBS] Corvos Hidalgo et al. 2022, *Educación Física y Ciencia* 24(2):e222: 30 experienced trainees, sets of 1–7 reps; Brzycki and O'Conner agreed best for squat; Epley, Brzycki, O'Conner, Cummings-Finn for bench; Cummings-Finn for deadlift; Cummings-Finn and Epley for military press. "No single equation performed best across all exercises"; match the equation to the exercise. — [Corvos Hidalgo et al. 2022](https://efyc.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/article/view/efyce222)
- [PRE] Marzagao (Fitbod), arXiv 2603.17495 (2026): 303,494 near-failure sets, 14,966 users, 388 exercises (from 37.7 M logged sets). Cited validation literature says accuracy degrades above ~10 reps (Mayhew et al. 2008: more accurate with <10 reps to failure); lower-body compounds and deadlift often show larger errors. Brzycki diverges as reps → 36, Epley grows linearly without bound. Proposed weight-dependent formula: 1RM = w × (1 + (r − 1)^0.85 / (−2.55 + 4.58·ln w)), which reduced inconsistency by 17–22% vs Brzycki/Epley/Wathen/Mayhew (16.3% better than Brzycki for compound, 21.9% for isolation). Caveats: no measured 1RM (optimized on within-user consistency over 14-day windows, so a formula that is consistently biased still scores well); "near-failure" inferred; bodyweight exercises excluded. — [Marzagao 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495)
- [OBS] Zhang 2021 note: 5 of 8 autoregulation studies used formula-estimated 1RM, flagged as a limitation. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)

**RIR / RPE accuracy**
- [MA, exploratory] Halperin et al. (preprint 2021; published in *Sports Medicine* 2022): 13 publications / 12 studies, 414 participants, 262 effects. Pooled: participants *underpredicted* reps to failure by 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17–1.73); between-person SD 1.45 reps; I² = 97.9%. Moderators: predictions closer to failure slightly more accurate (β = −0.025/rep, borderline); sets with >12 reps to failure much worse (β 0.47 vs 0.06 for <12); later sets trivially better (β −0.07); training experience no effect (β −0.006); upper vs lower body no clear difference (−0.58, CI −2.32 to 1.16). — [Halperin et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)
- [OBS] Remmert et al. 2023 (58 participants, cable exercises at 72.5% e1RM, 4 sets to failure): mean error 1.2 reps when predicting 5 RIR vs 0.46 at 1 RIR; set 1 0.96 vs set 3 0.71; sex, experience, and prior RIR use not significant. — [SBS 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- [OBS] Remmert et al. (9 trained men, bench, 6 weeks): error ~1.1 reps at 4 RIR vs ~0.7 at 1 RIR; each extra rep performed cut raw error by ~0.40 reps; accuracy did *not* improve over the 6 weeks. — [SBS 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- [OBS] Refalo et al. 2023 (24 trained, bench at 75% 1RM): mean absolute error 0.65 ± 0.78 reps; no sex/experience differences. — [SBS 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)
- [OBS] Emanuel, Har-Nir, Obolski, Halperin 2022 (259 coaches judging videos): absolute error 4.8 reps at 33% of the set, 2.0 at 66%, 1.2 at 90%; heavier load (80 vs 70% 1RM) reduced error by ~1.2 reps; second set better; coach experience negligible. — [Emanuel et al. 2022 preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/165)
- [EXP] Practical calibration advice (Androulakis-Korakakis, SBS 2024): assume you underpredict RIR by ~1 rep; occasionally take a last set to failure while predicting RIR to calibrate per exercise (anecdotal); prefer ≤12-rep sets if RIR is hard to judge at high reps; judge RIR near the end of the set, not at the start. — [SBS 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)

### Inferences
- For software: compute e1RM only from sets with low reps-to-failure, i.e. reps + RIR ≤ ~10–12. Treat high-rep sets (small exercises in 12–20+ ranges) as unreliable for e1RM and progress them on reps/load directly.
- RIR-adjusted e1RM: plug "effective reps to failure" = reps + RIR into the formula (e.g. Epley on reps+RIR). This is the standard practitioner approach [BK]. Given the ~1-rep underprediction and the 1.45-rep SD between people, logged RIR ≥4 should be treated as low-information. RIR 0–3 logs are usable.
- Because experience barely improves RIR accuracy but proximity to failure does, an app could ask for occasional calibration sets (to failure on safe exercises), rather than assuming accuracy improves with training age.
- [BK, unverified here] Standard definitions: Epley 1RM = w·(1 + r/30); Brzycki 1RM = w·36/(37 − r); Lombardi 1RM = w·r^0.10. Epley and Brzycki give nearly the same value around 10 reps. Lombardi gives lower estimates at high reps.

### Gaps
- Primary rep-range-specific error tables for Epley/Brzycki/Lombardi (e.g. LeSuer 1997, Reynolds 2006) were not retrieved. "Valid up to ~10 reps" is supported only via secondary citation in the arXiv preprint.
- No validation study of RIR-adjusted e1RM formulas against tested 1RM was found.
- No data found specific to small-muscle exercises (neck, wrist, toes, hip rotators) for either e1RM or RIR accuracy. Note: Emanuel found preacher curls judged *more* accurately than squats in one analysis but higher error in another reported coefficient, so the source is internally ambiguous on exercise type.

## 3. Load increment rules, microloading, rep-range width, and evidence on double progression

### Takeaway
There is almost no experimental evidence on increment size or rep-range width. The closest trial (Plotkin 2022) shows that progressing reps at a fixed load and progressing load within an 8–12 range give near-identical hypertrophy, with a small (~2 kg, CI crossing zero) strength edge for adding load. Increment rules are therefore coaching convention.

### Cited Findings
- [RCT] Plotkin et al. 2022, *PeerJ* 10:e14142: 43 trained adults (38 completers), 8 weeks, 2×/week, 4 sets each of squat, leg extension, 2 calf raises, all to momentary failure. LOAD group added load to stay in 8–12; REPS group kept load and added reps. Smith squat 1RM +21.8 kg (LOAD) vs +19.3 kg (REPS); adjusted difference ~2 kg favouring LOAD (90% CI ≈ −2.4 to 7.8). Endurance equal. Hypertrophy similar except rectus femoris favouring REPS (+2.8 mm, preliminary). The exact load-increment rule was not specified on the page. — [Plotkin et al. 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142)
- [EXP] Rippetoe / *Practical Programming*, as paraphrased by a forum user: "microload when you start wondering whether you should." — [Starting Strength forum](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html)
- [EXP] StrongLifts app lets users set per-exercise increments (support article listed). — [StrongLifts support](https://support.stronglifts.com/article/71-increments) (content not fetched)

### Inferences
- Plotkin 2022 is the closest evidence for double progression. Both of its halves (add reps, then add load) work, and loading matters more for 1RM. This backs a double-progression rule whose load step is triggered by reaching the top of the range.
- A relative increment rule (≈2–5% of working load, rounded to the smallest available plate) scales across a 100-movement program better than fixed absolute jumps. A 2.5 kg jump is ~2% on a 120 kg squat but 25–50% on a 5–10 kg wrist or neck exercise. For tiny loads, rep progression or longer rep ranges has to stand in for microloading. This is reasoning, not tested evidence.
- Rep-range width interacts with increment size. The top of the range should allow at least the reps lost when load rises by one increment (roughly 1 rep per ~2.5–3% load at moderate reps, from the shape of e1RM formulas). Low-rep compounds (e.g. 3–6) tolerate ~2–5% jumps. Small exercises with large relative jumps need wide ranges (e.g. 10–20 or 12–25). This is derived from formula arithmetic, not an empirical study.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on optimal increment size, microloading, or rep-range width.
- No study found evaluating "double progression counted per session" vs per set.

## 4. Stall detection and resets: where does "3 stalls then −10%" come from?

### Takeaway
"Fail the same weight about 3 sessions in a row, then cut the load by ~10% and build back up" is coaching convention from novice linear-progression programs (Rippetoe's *Starting Strength / Practical Programming*, copied by StrongLifts and apps such as openGym). No study has validated the stall threshold or the deload size.

### Cited Findings
- [EXP] Practical Programming stall protocol (forum paraphrase): on first missed reps, retry the weight 2–3 times. After missing reps for 3 workouts in a row, "deload 10% (not 20)" and resume 3×5, with no AMRAP on deload weights. Never drop below 90% of previous 5RMs when deloading. If stalls recur, switch to intermediate programming (more variation and less frequent PRs). — [Starting Strength forum, citing Practical Programming](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html)
- [EXP] Critiques of repeated resets exist in the coaching community (e.g. "Rethinking the re-set"); not fetched. — [Deep Squats Shallow Thoughts](https://deepsquatsshallowthoughts.substack.com/p/rethinking-the-re-set)
- [MA] Autoregulation literature does not test stall/reset rules. APRE avoids explicit stall logic because every session adjusts load up or down from performance. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)

### Inferences
- With RIR logged, a stall detector can be better than "3 sessions at the same load." Track e1RM (from low-rep sets) or reps at a fixed load and RIR. Flag a stall when e1RM fails to rise over N exposures *and* RIR is not falling. That separates a true plateau from a "same reps but easier" session. With irregular frequency, counting exposures rather than calendar time is appropriate.
- The −10% reset is roughly one to three typical increments for compounds, enough to give a few sessions of re-progression. For low-load small exercises, −10% may be smaller than one plate step. Use "drop one increment, or reduce the rep target" there instead.
- For a lifter returning after a break, early "stalls" are more likely mis-set starting loads or fatigue from kung fu than true plateaus. A rule that first checks RIR or fatigue context before deloading would avoid premature resets.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found on how many failed sessions define a stall, or on the optimal deload percentage, reset vs variant swap. The Practical Programming text itself was not accessed, only a forum paraphrase.
- 5/3/1 (Wendler) "reset training max 10%" and similar rules were not verified in this session.

## 5. Proximity to failure for strength (RIR 0 vs 1–3 vs more)

### Takeaway
For maximal strength, how close sets go to failure barely matters across a wide RIR range. Load (%1RM) matters more. Hypertrophy improves modestly as sets approach failure. Training to failure every set is unnecessary for strength.

### Cited Findings
- [MA, meta-regression] Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele, Zourdos 2024, *Sports Medicine* 54(9):2209–2231, doi 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2. Strength: 243 effects / 55 studies; best-fit slope per RIR β = 0.003 (95% CI −0.012 to 0.018), so the relationship is negligible. Hypertrophy: 140 effects / 26 studies; β = −0.019 SMC per RIR (−0.035 to −0.004), so growth increases closer to failure. Load appears a better predictor of strength gain than RIR. Authors warn against strong prescriptions because RIR was estimated post hoc, model fit was modest, and intervals were wide. — [Robinson et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654); [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4089104?lng=en)
- [MA] Refalo et al. 2023, *Sports Medicine* 53(3):649–665 (15 studies): failure vs non-failure hypertrophy ES = 0.19 (0.00–0.37), a trivial advantage. Momentary-failure subgroup ES 0.12 (ns). Volume load and relative load did not moderate. — [Refalo et al. 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full)
- [MA] Hickmott 2022: ≤25% velocity loss (i.e. stopping further from failure) was better for 1RM than >25% (MD 2.32 kg). — [Hickmott et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)
- [RCT] Helms 2018 RPE group trained at RPE 5–9 (1–5 RIR) and gained strength equal to or slightly more than the %1RM group. — [SBS 2024](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve)

### Inferences
- For a strength-focused program with heavy concurrent kung fu load, working sets at ~1–3 RIR (compounds) are supported: strength cost ≈ 0, less fatigue. Occasional 0-RIR sets on small or safe exercises are useful for calibrating RIR (section 2) and slightly benefit hypertrophy.
- For software: a target RIR band (e.g. 1–3) can gate progression. "Hit top of rep range at RIR ≥1" means progress the load. "Hit it only at RIR 0" means hold the load. This makes double progression less sensitive to grinding.

### Gaps
- Robinson 2024 did not report predicted effects at specific RIR values or a load × RIR interaction for strength in the main model.
- Refalo 2023 strength outcomes were not available in the fetched page. (Refalo published a separate 2024 failure-vs-RIR trial; not retrieved.)

## 6. Which of these are evidence-based vs coaching convention?

### Takeaway
Evidence-based: autoregulation (RPE/RIR, APRE, VBT) is at least as effective as fixed %1RM, with a possible small advantage. RIR is accurate to about ±1 rep near failure and worse far from it. Proximity to failure barely affects strength. Adding reps vs adding load are both viable. Convention: double-progression parameters, increment sizes, rep-range widths, stall definitions, and the −10% reset.

### Cited Findings
- Evidence-based [MA]: autoregulation ≥ fixed loading for strength. SMD 0.64 favouring it (Zhang 2021) vs ns MD 2.07 kg (Hickmott 2022). — [Zhang 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full); [Hickmott 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)
- Evidence-based but low certainty [MA/NMA]: APRE ranks first, driven by few, partly non-randomized trials. — [JESF 2025](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4094556); [Larsen 2021](https://peerj.com/articles/10663)
- Evidence-based [MA]: RIR underprediction ≈ 0.95 reps, better near failure and at ≤12 reps; experience has no effect. — [Halperin et al.](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)
- Evidence-based [MA]: strength is flat across RIR, hypertrophy improves near failure. — [Robinson et al. 2024](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654)
- Evidence-based, single trial [RCT]: rep vs load progression comparable. — [Plotkin 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/14142)
- Convention [EXP]: 3 failed sessions then −10%, floor at 90% of prior 5RM. — [Starting Strength forum](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-programming/34107-intermediate-programming-presses-2-print.html)
- Convention [EXP]: APRE adjustment table values (+5–10 lb etc.). — [Long Term Training](https://longtermtraining.substack.com/p/apre-removing-the-guesswork-for-strength)

### Inferences
- The user's chosen rule (double progression per session plus stall-triggered ~10% deload) does not conflict with the evidence. Its core (progress on performance) is evidence-aligned. Its parameters are convention, and the RIR data he already logs could add (a) an RIR gate on load increases, (b) e1RM-based stall detection for low-rep compounds, and (c) APRE-style variable jump sizes (bigger jump when the top of the range is far exceeded).
- Concrete implementable APRE-like rule for double progression (derived, not tested): if reps on the last work set exceed the top of the range by ≥3 at RIR ≥1, add two increments. If they reach the top, add one. If they land inside the range, hold the load. If below the bottom for 2–3 exposures with RIR ≤1, deload one or two increments (≈5–10%).

### Gaps
- No study evaluated full software progression algorithms such as double-progression engines, app auto-progression, or Fitbod-style models against outcomes.
- No evidence specific to returning-after-layoff lifters or to concurrent martial-arts training was searched (out of scope).
