# Training and progressing strength in small or unusual joint actions

Research-process caveat for the report writer: PubMed and PMC full texts returned reCAPTCHA pages, the Europe PMC API was blocked by the egress proxy (403) and then rate-limited through WebFetch (429), and the JOSPT page failed to load. Only a small number of primary abstracts could be verified in this session. Every item under "Cited Findings" was verified on the linked page. Items marked **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** under Gaps come from the researcher's background knowledge and were NOT confirmed in this session. Treat them as leads to check, not as citable facts.

Evidence labels: SR = systematic review, NMA = network meta-analysis, RCT = randomized controlled trial, CT = non-randomized controlled trial, PP = pre-post with no control, Narr = narrative review, EO = expert opinion.

## 1. Neck (flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation, retraction)

### Takeaway
Neck strength is clearly trainable. Small trials report about +17–21% isometric gains in 5 weeks of twice-weekly isometric work, and gains are smaller with low intensity or low frequency. No direct evidence was found that strengthening prevents concussion. The best evidence is small, unblinded or retrospective and comes only from male rugby and American football players. Rotation and retraction are almost unstudied as training targets.

### Cited Findings
- SR (Daly, Pearce & Ryan 2021, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 6(1):8). Out of 2,462 hits only **3 studies** met the criteria, all in adult male rugby union or American football players, all rated "neutral" quality, and none meta-analysed. — [Daly et al. 2021, PMC7838928](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)
- Geary et al. 2014 (CT; 15 professional rugby players vs 10 controls). Isometric protocol: manual resistance from a coach through a head harness, lying supine on a bench. **10-s holds at neutral, 3 holds per direction** (flexion, extension, left and right lateral flexion), **2x/week for 5 weeks**. Gains appeared in the intervention group only: flexion 334→396 N (~+18%), extension 606→734 N (~+21%), left lateral 556→657 N (~+18%), right lateral 570→668 N (~+17%). The percentages were calculated by the extraction tool from the reported means. — [Daly et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)
- Naish et al. 2013 (retrospective, no control; 27 professional rugby players). Isometric work on a cable machine and a scrum machine, with axial rotation excluded. 13-week strengthening phase followed by 13-week maintenance; sets and load not reported. Week-5 strength changes were not significant. Match cervical injuries fell from 11 to 2 (p=0.03), but total cervical injuries did not differ significantly (12 vs 6, p=0.34). — [Daly et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)
- Versteegh et al. 2019 (pilot, n≈8–10). Dynamic training with a helmet-mounted rotating 125 g weight, **6 sets × 50 revolutions in each direction, 2x/week for 7 weeks, ~10 min per session**, on top of a 4-way neck machine. Composite strength rose +32 N vs +12 N in controls, with a 46 N between-group difference in **axial rotation** (95% CI 9–83). This is one of the few data points on rotation. — [Daly et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)
- The SR found **no adverse events reported**. Isometric training was preferred on the assumption that it puts less load on discs and facets. This is a rationale, not a tested finding. The review concluded there is no evidence that neck strengthening reduces concussion or cervical injury in adults. — [Daly et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838928/)
- Narr (Sports Medicine 2016; 46(8):1111–1124). Neck programmes "were generally effective for both untrained and trained" people, using dynamic or isometric exercises and various devices. The magnitude of gains varied widely, and **the smallest gains came from programmes with lower intensity or frequency**. Evidence that greater strength improves head stabilisation is limited. A prospective high-school study linked greater overall isometric neck strength to lower concussion risk (an association, not a training effect). — [Sports Med 2016 review abstract (SPONET)](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4040015?lng=en)

### Inferences
- A practical evidence-aligned starting point is the protocol from the Geary study: isometric holds against a harness or band in 4 directions, 3 × 10 s per direction at a hard but controlled effort, 2x/week. Dynamic harness or plate work can be added once the isometrics feel easy. No trial compared harness training with a 4-way machine directly.
- Rotation and retraction (chin tuck) have almost no strength-training literature. They can be loaded, for example rotation via a cable with a head harness or manual isometrics, and retraction isometrically against a band. Expect gains to be documented less well, and progress them more conservatively.
- For a combat athlete, an association between strength and concussion exists, but causal protection is unproven. Strength is a reasonable but uncertain goal.

### Gaps
- No verified dose–response data (sets × load) beyond the few protocols above. No verified data on neck strength in combat sports.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Collins et al. 2014 (J Prim Prev): in high-school athletes, each 1 lb increase in neck strength was linked to about 5% lower concussion odds. This is likely the "prospective high-school study" cited above.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Mansell et al. 2005 (J Athl Train): 8 weeks of neck training increased isometric strength in soccer players but did not change head–neck kinematics during perturbation.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Conley et al. 1997 (Spine): 12 weeks of 4-way neck-machine training produced roughly 30%+ extension strength gains and neck muscle hypertrophy.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Measurement: hand-held dynamometry and fixed-frame or load-cell setups for isometric neck strength show good test–retest reliability in the literature. A cheap analogue is a crane or luggage scale attached to a head harness, anchored to a rack with the body braced. No verified ICCs for that DIY setup.

## 2. Shoulder rotators and scapular muscles (ER/IR, serratus anterior, lower trapezius, scapular elevation/depression/protraction/retraction, upward/downward rotation, tilts)

### Takeaway
ER strength responds to dedicated training. One verified study shows +24% eccentric ER strength after 6 weeks of eccentric training, and one RCT in youth handball found no added benefit over usual sport training. Scapular *strength* per se (tilt, downward rotation) has almost no strength-outcome literature. It is mostly studied as EMG activation or motor control. Protraction, retraction, elevation and depression can be loaded and progressed like any other lift.

### Cited Findings
- Vetter et al. 2023, *PLoS ONE* 18(12):e0293439 (PP, no control; n=16 active men). **Eccentric isokinetic ER training** at 30°/s, prone at 90° abduction and 90° elbow flexion, **2x/week for 6 weeks (12 sessions)**, with range and load increased after week 2. Results: eccentric ER strength **+24%** (p=.008), mostly at the end of the range; concentric ER up to +7% (not significant); IR unchanged. Supraspinatus fascicle length +16% and volume +19%, with no change in infraspinatus. Active ROM dropped 7% and passive ROM 4%. — [Vetter et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0293439)
- An RCT in youth handball titled "No Added Benefit of 8 Weeks of Shoulder External Rotation Strength Training for Youth Handball Players Over Usual Handball Training Alone" (JOSPT 2021). Only the title was verified. The page failed to load, so the protocol and numbers are unavailable. — [JOSPT 2021](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2021.9957)
- An EMG study (Sci Rep 2025) examined how shoulder abduction angle and elastic-band resistance change muscle activity during ER exercises. This is activation evidence, not strength gains. Only the title was verified. — [Nature Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-34767-w)

### Inferences
- The ER and IR cable work available to the user (side-lying ER with a dumbbell, cable ER at 0° and 90° abduction) matches what studies use. The +24% eccentric gain shows that a slow eccentric emphasis is worth including.
- The decrease in active ROM after eccentric ER training is a reminder to keep full-range work in the program.
- Scapular tilt and downward rotation are best treated as **motor-control or positional** targets. Strength is measured indirectly, through loaded exercises such as rhomboid or levator work in shrug or row variants. No verified trial reports "posterior tilt strength" as an outcome.

### Gaps
- No verified protocol data for serratus anterior or lower trapezius strength trials.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Andersson et al. 2017 (BJSM): the OSTRC shoulder injury prevention program in elite handball (elastic-band ER, scapular and thoracic work, 3x/week warm-up) reduced shoulder problems by roughly 28%.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Measurement: hand-held dynamometry of shoulder ER/IR at 0° and 90° abduction has good reliability (ICC often >0.85). A cheap substitute is a crane scale on a cable or band handle. Scapular protraction strength can be tested with a supine "punch" isometric against a scale.

## 3. Forearm, wrist, grip, hand (pronation/supination, wrist flexion/extension/radial/ulnar deviation, crush/support/pinch grip, finger extension, finger ab/adduction, thumb extension/abduction)

### Takeaway
Grip and wrist flexion/extension are trainable, though few trials isolate them. Finger extensor, finger ab/adduction and thumb-specific strength training has almost no verified training literature, so trainability is assumed by physiology rather than demonstrated in trials. Wrist deviation and pronation/supination are trainable with offset-loaded implements but are rarely studied.

### Cited Findings
- A 12-week wrist and forearm training study in high-school baseball players exists ("Effect of 12 weeks of wrist and forearm training on high school baseball players"). The abstract could not be retrieved past a PubMed captcha, so outcomes are unverified. — [PubMed 15320673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15320673/)
- A study on the effect of forearm position on handgrip strength (*J Sport Sci Phys Educ*, UPSI) shows that measurement posture matters when testing grip. Only the title was verified. — [UPSI JSSPJ](https://ojs.upsi.edu.my/index.php/JSSPJ/article/view/585)

### Inferences
- **Measurement is the strong point here.** Crush grip can be measured cheaply with a digital hand dynamometer or a crane scale on a handle. Standardised posture (elbow at 90°, forearm neutral) matters, as the UPSI study title implies.
- Pinch and support grip can be measured as time or load on a pinch block or plate pinch and on bar hangs or farmer holds. Finger extension can be measured as reps against a band of known tension, or with a crane scale looped over the fingertips.
- Pronation, supination and wrist deviation are commonly trained with a hammer or offset dumbbell held by the end of the handle. Progress them by moving the hand further from the weight, which increases the lever.

### Gaps
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Hand-dynamometer grip testing (Jamar-type) is among the most reliable strength tests, with ICC around 0.9+.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Grip-training studies in climbers and older adults show strength gains. Finger-extensor training trials in healthy adults are essentially absent; extensor band work is mainly expert-opinion "balance" advice from climbing and grip coaches.
- No verified data on thumb abduction or extension or on finger ab/adduction strength training.

## 4. Hip rotators, abductors and adductors (IR/ER in flexion and extension, abduction, adduction)

### Takeaway
Adductor strength is among the best-evidenced "unusual" targets. The Copenhagen Adduction exercise produced **+35.7% eccentric adduction strength in 8 weeks** in an RCT, and a programme based on it was associated with a 41% reduction in groin problems. Hip IR/ER are clearly loadable with cable-and-ankle-strap or machine setups, but verified training-outcome data for them were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Ishøi et al. 2016, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 26(11):1334–1342 (cluster RCT with blinded tester; n=24 U-19 sub-elite footballers). An **8-week supervised progressive Copenhagen Adduction programme** on top of usual training. Results: eccentric hip adduction strength **+35.7%** (p<0.001), eccentric abduction **+20.3%** (p=0.003), adduction/abduction ratio +12.3%. Controls showed no change. Compliance was 91%, and median muscle soreness was 0–2 out of 10. The abstract does not list sets and reps. — [Ishøi et al. 2016 (UCViden)](https://www.ucviden.dk/en/publications/large-eccentric-strength-increase-using-the-copenhagen-adduction-/)
- An editorial by Ishøi & Thorborg (BJSM) argues that **repetitions or time under tension** are the most practical way to progress the Copenhagen Adduction. It also cites Harøy et al.: a pre-season plus in-season adductor programme reduced groin problems by **41%** in football. — [BJSM editorial](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/19/1066)
- A low-dose Copenhagen Adduction intervention trial exists (University of Bath / Region H). Only the title was verified. — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/effects-of-a-lowdose-copenhagen-adduction-exercise-intervention-o/)
- A frame-stabilised dynamometer has been validated for hip strength assessment. Only the title was verified. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4083393?lng=en)
- A pilot study reports on test–retest reliability of isometric hip strength measured with hand-held dynamometry. Only the title was verified. — [AMSJ](https://www.amsj.org/?p=3933)

### Inferences
- Copenhagen Adduction has built-in progression: short lever (knee support) → long lever (ankle support) → more reps or longer holds → added load. This suits a returning lifter, and the RCT reported low soreness.
- Hip IR/ER can be trained in both positions the user listed. Seated (hip flexed): cable with ankle strap or seated machine rotation. Prone or standing (hip extended): cable or band. They can be progressed like any isolation lift. Measure with a crane scale or hand-held dynamometer, testing seated at 90° hip flexion with the thigh stabilised.

### Gaps
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Harøy et al. 2019 (BJSM; RCT, n≈650 footballers): the "Adductor Strengthening Programme" used Copenhagen Adduction progressions about 2–3x/week pre-season and 1x/week in-season, with 1 set of 3–15 reps per side.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Polglass et al. 2019 low-dose Copenhagen Adduction: about 1–2x/week with low volume still increased eccentric adduction strength.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Thorborg et al. (2010–2013) report good reliability for hip-strength hand-held dynamometry, including the "squeeze" test with a sphygmomanometer or dynamometer between the knees. A cheap adductor squeeze test is possible with a blood-pressure cuff.
- No verified trial with hip IR/ER isolated strength as the primary outcome was retrieved.

## 5. Lower leg and foot (dorsiflexion/tibialis, inversion, eversion, plantar flexion with knee straight vs bent, toe flexion/extension, toe abduction, arch/intrinsics, tibial rotation)

### Takeaway
Inversion and eversion strength are trainable, and an NMA exists for inversion function in chronic ankle instability. Intrinsic foot muscle and short-foot training improves function, but certainty is low to very low and a 2022 SR found no arch-kinematic effect. Toe-flexor strength is trainable, but key data were not retrievable. Toe abduction and tibial rotation are niche: tibial internal-rotator strength has at least one rehab study (post-ACL reconstruction), and toe abduction is largely a motor-control skill.

### Cited Findings
- A systematic review and network meta-analysis asked: "Which therapeutic exercise is most effective for improving ankle inversion muscle function in individuals with chronic ankle instability?" Only the title was verified; PMC was blocked. — [PMC12756897](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12756897)
- An RCT compared isokinetic rehabilitation with Thera-Band strength training in functional ankle instability. Only the title was verified. — [PMC9714719](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9714719/)
- An RCT on strength-training protocols for chronic ankle instability was published in *J Athl Train* 50(1):36. Only the title and venue were verified. — [NATA PDF](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/attr/50/1/article-p36.pdf)
- Willemse et al. 2022, *J Foot Ankle Res* 15:3 (SR, PROSPERO registered, 11 studies, n=226, mean age 23). The review covered **plantar intrinsic foot muscle (PIFM) strengthening**: 9 of the 11 studies used the **short-foot exercise**, with towel curls as a comparator in some. Interventions lasted **4–8 weeks at ≥3 sessions/week up to daily**, from a few minutes to 30 min per session, and most were progressive. Findings: low-certainty evidence for better foot function in gait, very low-certainty evidence for better dynamic balance (SMD 0.41–1.43), and **no evidence of an effect on medial arch kinematics**. — [Willemse et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13047-021-00509-0)
- A meta-analysis (ACFAS listing) covers the effects of the short-foot exercise on foot alignment and muscle hypertrophy in people with flatfoot. Only the title was verified. — [ACFAS](https://acfas.org/the-effects-of-the-short-foot-exercise-on-foot-alignment-and-muscle-hypertrophy-in-flatfoot-individu)
- A systematic review and meta-analysis, "Evidence of intrinsic foot muscle training in improving foot function," exists (Physio-Network summary). Only the title was verified. — [Physio-Network](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/ankle-foot/evidence-of-intrinsic-foot-muscle-training-in-improving-foot-function-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/)
- A study reports on the effect of physiotherapy on **tibial internal-rotator strength** in males after ACL reconstruction. This shows that tibial rotation strength is at least measured and trained in rehab. Only the title was verified. — [PMC3560515](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3560515/)
- A study on tibialis anterior activity and dorsiflexor strength according to toe posture (*J Musculoskelet Sci Technol*) indicates that toe position changes dorsiflexion testing. Only the title was verified. — [JKEMA](https://www.jkema.org/archive/view_article?pid=jkema-4-1-13)

### Inferences
- Dorsiflexion (tibialis raises, or a cable with ankle strap) and plantar flexion (standing calf raise with knee straight for gastrocnemius, seated calf raise with knee bent for soleus) are ordinary trainable lifts. Progress them with reps and load, and measure them with an rep-max or a crane scale.
- Inversion and eversion with a cable and ankle strap or a band are well established in ankle-sprain rehab and can be progressed by load. Inversion is the target of a dedicated NMA.
- The arch and short-foot exercise is best framed as **motor control plus modest strength**, given that arch kinematics did not change.
- Toe flexion is loadable, for example with toe curls against a band, a towel with a weight, or seated isometric toe presses on a scale. A **bathroom scale** gives a cheap toe-flexor measurement: press the toes down while seated.
- Toe abduction (spreading) and isolated tibial rotation have essentially **no healthy-population strength-training evidence**. Treat them as motor-control skills whose "strength" is measured only as a rep or hold quality score.

### Gaps
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Goldmann et al. 2013 (J Biomech / Eur J Appl Physiol): 7 weeks of isometric toe-flexor training at about 70–90% MVC on a custom dynamometer increased toe-flexor strength by roughly 60%.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Mickle et al. 2016 (older adults, toe-flexor strengthening RCT) reported gains in toe strength.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Taddei et al. 2020 (MSSE, RCT, recreational runners): an 8-week foot-strengthening programme reduced running injuries.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Ridge et al. 2019 (MSSE): walking in minimalist shoes for 6 months increased foot muscle size and strength comparably to a foot-training programme.
- **[UNVERIFIED POINTER]** Docherty et al. 1998 (J Athl Train): 6 weeks of elastic-band training increased eversion and dorsiflexion strength in functional ankle instability. Kaminski et al. 2003 found no strength change with band training. The evidence conflicts.
- Toe-flexor gain percentages, the inversion NMA results and the short-foot hypertrophy meta-analysis numbers could not be extracted because full texts were blocked.

## 6. Cross-cutting: "motor control only" vs trainable strength, progression for a returning lifter, cheap measurement

### Takeaway
Actions with large agonists and an external load that can be applied and measured clearly respond to progressive overload. These include neck flexion, extension and lateral flexion, shoulder ER/IR, hip adduction/abduction/rotation, plantar and dorsiflexion, inversion/eversion, grip and wrist flexion/extension. Scapular tilt and downward rotation, toe abduction, the foot arch and tibial rotation are mostly motor-control targets, with little or no healthy-population strength-gain evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Low-intensity or low-frequency programmes produce the smallest neck gains, which implies that intensity and frequency drive results. — [Sports Med 2016 review](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4040015?lng=en)
- The Copenhagen Adduction RCT showed large gains with low soreness (median 0–2) and 91% compliance, which supports a gradual progression from short lever to long lever. — [Ishøi 2016](https://www.ucviden.dk/en/publications/large-eccentric-strength-increase-using-the-copenhagen-adduction-/)
- Progressing by repetitions or time under tension is recommended as the practical progression for bodyweight small-muscle exercises. — [BJSM editorial](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/19/1066)
- Programmes for intrinsic foot muscles were mostly progressive, lasted 4–8 weeks at ≥3x/week, and adherence when unsupervised was 67–102%. — [Willemse 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13047-021-00509-0)
- Eccentric ER training reduced active ROM by 7%. — [Vetter 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0293439)

### Inferences
- A common cheap measurement setup is a **digital crane or luggage scale (≈€10–20) plus a strap**, anchored to a rack or cable column, for isometric pulls. It covers neck (harness), shoulder ER/IR, hip IR/ER/ab/adduction, ankle inversion/eversion/dorsiflexion and finger extension. Standardise joint angle, anchor point and lever arm, and take the best of 3 trials. A **bathroom scale** covers toe flexion. A **hand dynamometer** covers grip. Rep-maxes at a fixed load suit isotonic tracking.
- For a returning lifter, start isometrics at a submaximal effort (RPE ~6–7) for 1–2 weeks, then move to dynamic sets of 2–3 × 10–15 at 2x/week, progressing reps before load. The neck especially should progress slowly. This progression is inferred from the study protocols plus general resistance-training practice and was not tested directly.

### Gaps
- No verified head-to-head comparisons of isometric vs dynamic training for any of these small actions, and no verified ICCs for DIY crane-scale testing.
- No verified data on whether the "motor control" items (scapular tilt, toe abduction, tibial rotation) show measurable strength gains with loading in healthy adults.
