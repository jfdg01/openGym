# Dose-response of resistance training for strength: volume, frequency, intensity, session structure, fitting many exercises into limited time

Evidence labels used below: **[MA]** meta-analysis / meta-regression; **[SR]** systematic review without pooling; **[RCT]** randomized trial; **[OBS]** observational / mixed-methods / survey; **[EO]** expert opinion / narrative review. Population noted where known (T = trained, UT = untrained).

## 1. Weekly sets per muscle/exercise for strength: dose-response, plateau, minimal effective dose

### Takeaway
Strength rises with weekly volume but flattens much earlier than hypertrophy. In the largest analysis (Pelland et al., 67 studies), each extra weekly set was worth ~0.21% more strength, and gains stopped being detectably larger at about 4 fractional sets/week. Per session, the plateau was about 2 direct sets. A single hard set per week already beat non-training controls. Low weekly volumes (~3–6 heavy sets/week per lift) are enough to keep making strength progress. Most of the volume benefit for strength is captured below ~10 sets/week per lift or muscle.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA, mixed T/UT]** Pelland et al., "The resistance training dose response: meta-regressions exploring the effects of weekly volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and strength gains". Published in *Sports Medicine* 2026;56(2):481–505. Data: 67 studies, 2,058 participants (79% male, mean age ~25 y). Models were multi-level Bayesian meta-regressions adjusted for duration and training status. Volume showed a positive slope for both strength and hypertrophy (posterior probability 100%), with diminishing returns that were "more pronounced for strength". — [SPONET record of published version](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- **[MA, preprint version of the same paper]** Strength vs weekly fractional sets: 66 studies, 490 effects, 2,020 participants. The marginal slope was **0.21% per weekly set** [95% CrI 0.16–0.26]. The authors describe "strong diminishing returns and a functional plateau". The smallest detectable effect size (SDES) for strength was **3.96%**, derived from 124 studies with non-training controls. Efficiency tiers for strength were:
  - minimum effective dose = **1 set/week**
  - "higher efficiency" = 2 sets
  - "intermediate efficiency" = 3–4 sets
  - "lower efficiency" = 5+ sets, where added sets no longer consistently exceed the SDES

  Hypertrophy had no clear plateau, and its minimum effective dose was 4 sets/week. Mean volume in the strength studies was only **8.14 ± 6.23 fractional sets/week** at **1.97 ± 0.92 sessions/week**, so very high volumes are sparsely sampled. 28 studies used untrained and 39 trained participants. Training status was a covariate, and no separate T/UT curves were reported. — [Pelland et al. preprint, SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- **[MA, preprint]** Per-session volume (Remmert/Pelland group, SportRxiv 537, 2025). Direct counting best predicted strength, and fractional counting best predicted hypertrophy. The point of undetectable outcome superiority (PUOS) was **~2 direct sets per session for strength** and **~11 fractional sets per session for hypertrophy**. Strength showed steeper diminishing returns. The authors caution that "there is insufficient data with very high per-session set volumes". — [SportRxiv preprint 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **[OBS/mixed-methods, competitive powerlifters (highly T)]** Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 (*Front Sports Act Living*) proposed a minimum effective training dose (METD) for 1RM in powerlifters: **~3–6 working sets/week of 1–5 reps per lift**, spread over **1–3 sessions/week**, at **>80% 1RM, RPE 7.5–9.5**, for 6–12 weeks. Other options from the same paper:
  - Autoregulated single-rep sets at RPE 9–9.5 save the most time, but gains are "less likely to be meaningful".
  - Adding 2–3 back-off sets at ~80% of the single's load may help.
  - Accessories: 1–3 per lift at RPE 7–9, ~6–10 reps.
  - Studies 3–4 were small (n = 25), not pre-registered, and exploratory.
  - Lifters themselves considered a 6-week gain "meaningful" at about squat 7.1 kg, bench 4.4 kg and deadlift 8.1 kg.

  — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)
- **[SR, mostly UT/recreational]** The earlier systematic review by Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2019, as summarized in the 2021 paper above. **A single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week, to high effort, for 8–12 weeks** produced "suboptimal but statistically significant" squat and bench 1RM increases. There were no data on the deadlift, women, or highly strength-trained athletes. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)
- **[OBS pilot, powerlifters]** Lifters doing only **~2 heavy singles/week squat, 3 bench and 1 deadlift** improved peri-training 1RM. However, 3 of 5 had a lower competition total after 10 weeks. Bare-minimum doses can maintain or slightly raise strength, but may underperform over longer periods. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)
- **[MA]** The Pelland preprint cites a study in well-trained powerlifters where "1–3 direct weekly sets was insufficient" while "3–9" was sufficient. Trained lifters likely need more than the pooled minimum of 1 set. — [Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)

### Inferences
- **Practical strength targets per lift or muscle**, with "sets" meaning hard sets close to failure (RIR 0–3):
  - ~1–2 sets/week maintains, and gives small gains in undertrained or neglected qualities.
  - ~3–6 sets/week is the efficient zone for continued strength gain in a returning intermediate.
  - Beyond ~8–10 sets/week the strength return per set is small.

  These numbers are drawn from the Pelland efficiency tiers, the Androulakis-Korakakis METD, and the mean study volume of ~8 sets/week. They are an inference, not a prescription from any one paper.
- For ~100 tracked movements, the earlier strength plateau favors **many movements at low volume** over a few movements at high volume. This is especially true for small isolation qualities such as neck, wrist, rotators, ankle and toes, where 1–3 hard sets/week is probably enough to register measurable gains in a returning lifter. Larger compound lifts can get 3–6+ sets/week.
- The previous draft had ~130 sets/week with maintenance items at 1–2 sets/week. That fits the evidence for maintenance items. Main lifts at 3–6 direct sets/week fall inside the efficient strength zone.
- A returning lifter regains strength faster than true novel gains, a "muscle memory" effect. This was not directly researched here, so the low end of these ranges is likely enough early on.

### Gaps
- **Ralston et al. 2017** (*Sports Med* 47:2585–2601; [PubMed 28755103](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28755103/)) grouped volume as low (<5), medium (5–9) and high (10+) weekly sets. PubMed and PMC both blocked automated reading (captcha), and Europe PMC rate-limited the request, so its effect sizes could not be verified here. My recollection is that the low-vs-high difference was small but significant, favoring higher volume. Treat that as unverified.
- Separate trained-vs-untrained dose-response curves for strength were not reported in Pelland. No data exist on very high volumes (>20 sets/week) for strength.
- I found no study on dose-response for neck, rotator-cuff, wrist, hip-rotation, ankle or toe strength specifically. The curves come from mostly large-muscle and compound outcomes.

## 2. Frequency: does training a lift more often help strength beyond volume? Specificity and transfer

### Takeaway
Pelland finds that strength (unlike hypertrophy) rises with frequency, with diminishing returns. The model predicts ~12.7% gain at 1 session/week vs ~17.3% at 2 sessions/week, and returns diminish beyond that. Older volume-equated meta-analyses found no significant frequency effect, so much of the "frequency benefit" may be extra volume or extra practice of the tested lift. Strength is specific: it transfers partially to related lifts, and most to the lift actually trained.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA]** In the published Pelland et al. analysis, frequency had a 100% posterior probability of a positive effect on **strength**, with diminishing returns. For **hypertrophy** the effect was compatible with negligible. "Only strength shows consistently identifiable effects with frequency." — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- **[MA, preprint numbers]** Strength frequency slope: **3.27% per session/week** [2.74–3.84]. Predicted gain was **12.72% at 1 session/week** vs **17.32% at 2 sessions/week**, and "beyond this point, accelerating diminishing returns occur". For hypertrophy, the slope was 0.32% with only 91.3% probability of being >0. — [Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- **[MA, Grgic et al. 2018, Sports Med]**, as reported in Ralston et al. 2018. Effect sizes for strength rose with frequency: **0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and ≥4 days/week**. The **volume-equated subgroup showed no significant frequency effect (p = 0.421)**. — [Ralston et al. 2018, Sports Med Open](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)
- **[MA, Ralston et al. 2018, mostly UT: 8 untrained vs 4 trained cohorts, 299 subjects, 12 studies, only 2 RCTs]**
  - Overall, low (1×) vs high (3×+) frequency was not significant: ES 0.09, p = 0.25.
  - Volume-equated, low vs high frequency: ES 0.03, p = 0.78.
  - Upper body favored higher frequency: ES 0.48, p < 0.01.
  - Lower body: no difference.
  - Isolation-only exercises: no frequency advantage (ES −0.10, p = 0.56).
  - The authors conclude frequency benefits are mostly explained by greater total volume. Heterogeneity was high (I² = 82%).

  — [Ralston et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)
- **[EO / narrative review of RCTs]** On specificity and transfer, Mang, Kravitz & Beam 2022 (*Strength Cond J* 44(3):101–106) state that "strength gains are greatest in the trained exercise", while related untrained lifts also improve. Examples:
  - Squat and deadlift training each improved both lifts, more in the trained one (Nigro).
  - Rear-foot-elevated split squat raised back-squat 1RM by **10 kg** with no squatting (Speirs, 5 weeks).
  - Kettlebell swings raised deadlift 1RM by **8 kg** (Maulit, 4 weeks).
  - Squat training transferred to leg press more than the reverse (Wirth; Rossi).
  - Multi-joint training produced larger gains in untrained lifts than single-joint training did. Single-joint groups improved their own single-joint lifts more (Paoli; Stien).

  — [Mang et al. 2022, SCJ](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/3c0357cb66b54c6b9a598b0ca578600a/scj-transfer-between-lifts-2022.pdf)
- **[MA]** Exercise order also shows specificity. Strength gains are larger in whichever exercise is done first: multi-joint first gives ES 0.32 (p = 0.034) for multi-joint tests, and single-joint first gives ES 0.58 (p = 0.032) for single-joint tests. The authors frame this as the specificity principle. — [Nunes et al. 2021, Eur J Sport Sci, SPONET/LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059795?lng=en)
- **[MA]** Pelland's finding that **direct** counting best predicts strength (per session) also implies strength is specific to the measured movement. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)

### Inferences
- For strength measured *in a specific movement*, each movement needs **direct** practice. A compound lift will raise related isolation strength somewhat, but not fully. A squat will not fully express as leg-extension strength, and the leg-press example shows transfer is asymmetric. Each of the ~100 tracked actions therefore needs at least some direct exposure if the goal is measurable gains *in that action*.
- Frequency beyond about 2 exposures/week per movement brings small marginal gains (Pelland). Spreading a fixed weekly volume over 2 sessions is better than 1 for strength. 3+ exposures/week matter mainly for skill-heavy main lifts.
- With an irregular rotating queue, aim for each item to come up **≥1×/week** (minimum). Main lifts should come up **~2×/week**.

### Gaps
- Pelland's predicted gains for 3, 4 or more sessions/week are shown only in figures, not in the text read here.
- There are no frequency data specific to small-joint isolation work (neck, wrist, toes).
- Quantitative transfer coefficients between specific pairs, such as squat to leg-extension 1RM, were not found. Evidence is per-study and heterogeneous.

## 3. Intensity / load and rep ranges for strength

### Takeaway
For maximal (1RM) strength, heavier loads beat light loads. Moderate (9–15RM) and heavy (≤8RM) loads differ only slightly, while hypertrophy is load-independent. Use heavy, low-rep work (≥80% 1RM, 1–6 reps) for lifts where 1RM is the test, and moderate loads for isolation and small-joint work where 1RM testing is impractical.

### Cited Findings
- **[Network MA, 28 studies, 747 healthy adults, sets to volitional failure]** Lopez et al. 2021, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 53(6):1206–1216. Loads were defined as high ≤8RM, moderate 9–15RM and low >15RM.
  - Strength: high vs low gave SMD 0.60–0.63, and moderate vs low gave SMD 0.34–0.35 (p < .001–.003).
  - **High vs moderate gave SMD 0.26–0.28, which was not significant (p = .068).**
  - Hypertrophy did not differ between loads.
  - Trained participants gained more muscle with more sessions.

  — [Lopez et al. 2021, JYX repository](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)
- **[OBS/EO, powerlifters]** For 1RM maintenance or gain at minimal dose, the authors recommend **>80% 1RM, 1–5 reps, RPE 7.5–9.5**. Single heavy reps at RPE 9–9.5 alone save the most time but are less likely to bring meaningful gains. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)
- **[SR]** Moderate loads also work at very low dose: a single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week, increased squat and bench 1RM. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 (citing their 2019 SR)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)

### Inferences
- **Main compound lifts tested by 1RM or e1RM:** mostly 3–6 reps at RPE 7–9, with an occasional heavy single.
- **Isolation and small-joint actions** (neck, rotators, wrist, ankle, toes, hip rotation): 6–15 reps near failure is appropriate. Heavy 1–3RM loading is impractical and riskier for these, and hypertrophy, which underlies strength, is load-independent. Track progress with an rep-based estimated 1RM (e1RM) or with reps at a fixed load.
- Because heavy and moderate loads differ for strength by only SMD ~0.27 (not significant), rep ranges can be chosen for safety and time without much loss.

### Gaps
- Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (low vs high load meta-analysis) and Schoenfeld et al. 2021 ("repetition continuum" review) were not fetched in this session, so their numbers are not reported here. Lopez 2021 is the more recent and larger analysis.

## 4. Fractional counting of indirect volume (compound vs isolation overlap)

### Takeaway
Supported, with one nuance. Counting indirect sets as 0.5 best predicted outcomes in the weekly model, especially hypertrophy. For **strength in a specific measured exercise**, direct-only counting performed best in the per-session model. Count compound sets at 0.5 toward synergist muscles for muscle-level volume, but give each tested movement its own direct sets.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA]** Pelland et al. compared three ways of weighting indirect sets: total (1.0), fractional (0.5) and direct (0). "The fractional counting method had the strongest support". The authors conclude that "separating direct and indirect sets appears important." — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- **[MA, preprint]** In per-session modeling, **direct** counting gave the strongest evidence for **strength**, while fractional counting was best for hypertrophy. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **[RCT-based EO]** Multi-joint training improves untrained single-joint lifts, but single-joint groups gain more in their own single-joint lifts. That is consistent with partial (fractional) transfer. — [Mang et al. 2022](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/3c0357cb66b54c6b9a598b0ca578600a/scj-transfer-between-lifts-2022.pdf)

### Inferences
- Suggested counting rule for the app:
  - **Muscle-level volume:** 1.0 for prime movers and 0.5 for meaningful synergists. Examples: bench counts 0.5 for triceps and front delt, and row counts 0.5 for biceps and rear delt.
  - **Movement-level strength:** only direct sets of that movement or a near-identical variant count.

  Maintenance targets for isolation items can then be met partly by compounds for muscle-level volume, but not for movement-specific strength.
- For small structures rarely loaded by compounds (neck, toes, wrist rotation, hip rotation), indirect volume is ~0, so they need direct sets.

### Gaps
- No study validates 0.5 as the exact coefficient for any specific muscle pair. It is a modeling convention that fit best among three options.

## 5. Session design: exercise order, supersets/antagonist pairing, rest intervals, full-body vs split

### Takeaway
- Put the movements whose strength matters most first, because the first exercises gain the most.
- Antagonist or non-competing supersets cut session time and do not hurt long-term strength or hypertrophy.
- Rest ≥2 min between heavy sets of main lifts for trained lifters. Shorter rests (60–120 s) are fine for isolation work.
- Full-body and split routines give similar strength gains when volume is equated.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA, 11 studies]** Exercise order (Nunes et al. 2021, *Eur J Sport Sci* 21(2):149–157). Combined, there was no order effect (ES −0.11, p = 0.306). Multi-joint lifts gained more when done first (ES 0.32, p = 0.034), and single-joint lifts gained more when done first (ES 0.58, p = 0.032). Hypertrophy was unaffected (ES 0.03, p = 0.862). — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059795?lng=en)
- **[MA, 19 studies, 313 participants]** Supersets vs traditional sets (Zhang, Weakley, Li, Li, García-Ramos et al. 2025, *Sports Med*, doi 10.1007/s40279-025-02176-8).
  - Acute, per session: repetitions (SMD −0.03) and volume load (SMD 0.05) were unchanged. Session duration was shorter and **training efficiency was higher (SMD 1.74, p = 0.01)**. RPE was higher (SMD 0.77), and lactate and energy cost were higher.
  - **Chronic:** maximal strength (SMD 0.10, p = 0.36) and hypertrophy (SMD −0.05) showed no difference.
  - Subtypes: **agonist–antagonist pairs increased reps completed (SMD 0.68, p = 0.01)**, while pairs of similar movements **reduced volume load (SMD −1.08, p < 0.01)**.

  — [Leeds Beckett repository](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)
- **[SR, 23 studies, 491 participants]** Rest intervals (Grgic et al. 2018, *Sports Med* 48(1):137–151).
  - In **trained** individuals, "robust gains" occurred even with <60 s rest, but **>2 min appears necessary to maximize strength**.
  - In **untrained** individuals, **60–120 s** appears sufficient.

  — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4047011)
- **[RCT, UT men, n = 67, 8 weeks, volume equated at 16 sets/muscle/week]** Full-body 4×/week (4 sets/session) vs split 2×/week (8 sets/session), Evangelista et al. 2021 (*einstein*):
  - 1RM bench: +17.5% full-body vs +18.1% split.
  - 1RM squat: +28.6% vs +28.2%.
  - Muscle thickness: similar.
  - No group-by-time interaction.

  — [Evangelista et al. 2021](https://www.scielo.br/j/eins/a/sQYb5T8zwYNzgNCpK8pDYwF/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- **Session template for 60–75 min:**
  1. 1–2 main compound lifts first, heavy, with 2–3 min rest. Antagonist pairing is acceptable here (e.g., press with row).
  2. Then supersets or tri-sets of non-competing isolation and small-joint items with ~60–90 s between exercises. Examples: neck plus calf raise plus wrist curl; external rotation plus hip rotation.

  Avoid supersetting two exercises that load the same muscles, because volume load drops.
- **35-min version:** 1 main lift, plus 2–3 antagonist or non-competing supersets, plus 1 circuit of 3–4 maintenance items at 1 set each.
- Rotate which items go first across sessions, so that order favors different movements over time.
- Full-body sessions in a rotating queue are evidence-compatible. Weekly volume and exposure count matter more than the split format.

### Gaps
- **Ramos-Campo et al. 2024** (split vs full-body meta-analysis) did not surface in searches and was not verified. I recall a JSCR systematic review that found no difference, but it is not confirmed here.
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2019** (split vs full-body in trained men) was not fetched.
- Evidence on interference between concurrent martial-arts training and lifting was out of scope.

## 6. How many distinct exercises per session/week; minimal-dose strategies for many movements

### Takeaway
No study tests "how many distinct exercises can be trained productively". The available evidence suggests the binding limits are time and fatigue, not a cap on exercise count. Strength is specific, needs few direct sets (1–4/week) to register gains, saturates at ~2 direct sets per session, and supersets save time without hurting outcomes. A minimal-dose, high-breadth design is therefore well supported in principle.

### Cited Findings
- **[MA, preprint]** For strength, about 2 direct sets per session is the point beyond which extra sets no longer show detectable extra gain. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **[MA, preprint]** For strength, 1 set/week is the minimum effective dose, and 2–4 sets/week is the most efficient range. — [Pelland preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- **[MA]** Supersets keep volume and chronic strength outcomes while raising efficiency (SMD 1.74). — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)
- **[OBS/EO]** Minimal-dose powerlifting models use 1–3 accessories per main lift at 6–10 reps, RPE 7–9. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/full)
- **[MA]** Exercises placed later in a session gain less strength. With many exercises per session, those at the end are disadvantaged. — [Nunes et al. 2021](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059795?lng=en)

### Inferences
- **Time budget (inference).** A hard set plus its share of rest takes about 2.5–3.5 min when done straight, or about 1.5–2 min effective per set when supersetted.
  - A 60–75 min session fits ~20–30 working sets, so ~10–18 distinct exercises at 1–3 sets each.
  - A 35 min session fits ~10–14 sets.
- **Weekly coverage of ~100 items:**
  - ~15–20 main compounds at 3–6 sets/week ≈ 60–100 sets.
  - ~80 isolation and small-joint items at ~1 set/week ≈ 80 sets.
  - The total is ~140–180 sets/week if every item is hit weekly.

  At 3 sessions/week × ~25 sets, the budget is ~75 sets. Either most items must rotate on a **2–3 week cycle**, giving ~0.33–0.5 sets/week each, or many items must be grouped into multi-joint drills. Over a multi-week window, 1 hard set every 1–2 weeks is plausibly enough to maintain strength, and slightly more to gain it. That is an extrapolation from the 1 set/week minimum effective dose; frequencies below weekly are not directly studied.
- Prioritize queue placement by "staleness" (time since last exposure) and by the item's weekly target. Put items with the largest deficit early in the session.
- The ~130 sets/week target in the previous draft fits evidence-based efficiency only if the user actually trains ~4–5 days/week. On 1–2-day weeks the queue should cover main lifts first and let maintenance items slide.

### Gaps
- I found no study on training very large exercise menus (>20 distinct exercises/week) or on frequencies below once a week for maintaining isolated small-joint strength.
- I found no evidence on systemic fatigue limits when stacking many low-volume isolation sets, nor on interaction with 4 nights/week of kung fu.
