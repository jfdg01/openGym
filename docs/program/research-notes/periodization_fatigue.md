# Periodization, Deloads and Fatigue Management for Long-Term Strength (Irregular Schedules)

Evidence labels used: [MA] meta-analysis / systematic review with pooling; [SR] systematic review without full pooling; [RCT] randomized trial; [Trial] non-randomized or conference trial; [Delphi] structured expert consensus; [EO] expert opinion / coaching convention; [Thesis] doctoral thesis / methods work.

Note on access: PubMed, PMC and Europe PMC were blocked (captcha / proxy) during this research, so some numbers come from publisher pages, repository PDFs or reputable secondary summaries; this is flagged where it applies.

## Q1. Periodization models (linear vs DUP/WUP vs block vs flexible/autoregulated): does the model matter for intermediates?

### Takeaway
Periodized training beats non-periodized training for 1RM by a small margin (ES roughly 0.2-0.4). Undulating beats linear by a small margin, and only in trained people. Autoregulated loading beats fixed loading by a small-to-moderate margin in a few small studies. Weekly volume, frequency and adherence matter more than which model you pick, so a flexible undulating or autoregulated scheme suits an irregular lifter fine.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Williams et al. 2017 (Sports Med): 18 studies, 81 effects, 612 participants (1988-2015). Periodized vs non-periodized 1RM: ES 0.43 (95% CI 0.27-0.58). The nested-effects model gives ES 0.38 (0.09-0.66). After removing outliers, ES 0.23 (0.13-0.33) with no heterogeneity left. Egger's test suggests publication bias (P=0.006). — [Williams et al. 2017 PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf)
- [MA] Williams 2017 moderators: undulating > linear (b=0.51, P=0.001), but six studies with an unspecified model were coded as linear. Untrained participants gained more than trained (b=-0.59). Longer studies gained more (+0.03 ES per week), and higher weekly frequency gained more (+0.46 per session/week). Volume was not a significant predictor. Most studies lasted under 16 weeks. — [Williams et al. 2017 PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf)
- [MA] Moesgaard et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52(7):1647-1666): 35 RCTs, 1,187 participants, 6-36 weeks, 2-4 sessions/week, volume-equated. Periodized > non-periodized for 1RM (ES ~0.31). No hypertrophy difference (ES ~0.13, negligible). Undulating > linear for strength (ES ~0.31), an effect seen in trained subjects (~0.61) but nearly absent in untrained (~0.06). Block periodization was not meta-analysed. — [Sci-Sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/effects-intensity-volume-periodization-strength-hypertrophy-238/); [Examine summary](https://examine.com/faq/are-periodized-training-programs-superior-for-strength-gains) (calls the effects "trivial to small"). Effect sizes come from these secondary summaries, since PubMed was not reachable.
- [MA] Zhang et al. 2021 (Front Physiol 12:651112), autoregulation vs fixed loading: 8 studies, 166 athletes (151 men), 5-10 weeks. Overall 1RM SMD 0.64 (0.43-0.85); squat +4.65 kg, bench +3.21 kg. By method: APRE SMD 0.78 (significant), RPE 0.17 (NS, 15 participants), VBT 0.43 (NS). The difference between methods was not significant (P=0.08). The studies were small and unblinded, with fair-to-good PEDro scores, possible publication bias and internal reporting inconsistencies. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- [MA] Training frequency (Sports Med Open 2018; 12 studies, 299 subjects): volume-equated, 1 vs ≥3 days/week gave similar 1RM gains (ES diff 0.01, P=0.78). The authors conclude that weekly volume, not frequency per se, drives strength gains, but that "it remains unclear whether RT frequency on its own has effects." Upper-body strength favoured higher frequency when volume was not equated (ES 0.48, P<0.01). — [Sports Medicine-Open 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)

### Inferences
- For an intermediate lifter, the choice between periodization models gives a small edge, about 0.2-0.3 SD in 1RM. Turning up irregularly costs more than that edge. A rotating queue of full-body sessions that each have a different rep zone (heavy / medium / light) is effectively flexible DUP. It keeps the small undulating advantage and needs no calendar.
- Calendar-free design: keep a "next session" queue where the sessions differ in intensity (e.g. A = 3-5 reps, B = 6-8, C = 10-12). Progress each exercise-and-zone pair independently, so missed days never break the model.
- Autoregulation such as an RIR cap or APRE-style "reps achieved adjust next load" is supported by small studies. An RIR target per set (e.g. stop at 1-3 RIR) is the cheapest to implement in an app.

### Gaps
- Harries et al. 2015 (linear vs undulating meta-analysis, reportedly no significant difference) could not be retrieved or verified; do not cite numbers for it.
- No meta-analysis directly tests block periodization against others in non-athlete intermediates.
- Primary Moesgaard effect sizes were not verified against the PubMed abstract (access blocked).
- Daily vs weekly undulating was not separated in the sources reached.

## Q2. Deloads: evidence, consensus, definitions, triggers, planned vs reactive

### Takeaway
Deloads rest mostly on expert consensus, not trials. The only RCT, which used complete cessation, found a 1-week deload mid-program gave no extra growth and slightly reduced lower-body strength. Experts agree deloads can be planned or autoregulated, and should reduce volume (sets, reps, days) more than intensity.

### Cited Findings
- [Delphi] Bell et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open), "Integrating Deloading into Strength and Physique Sports Training Programmes". Panel: 34 coaches in round 1, 29 in round 2, 21 in round 3. Consensus threshold ≥70%. — [SHU repository PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf)
  - Definition: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue," promote recovery and prepare for the next cycle. Deloading is not meant to raise performance. It differs from tapering, because tapers come only before competition (95.2%).
  - Volume: reduce sets (100%), reps (100%) and training days (100%), possibly to a "minimum effective dose" (90.5%). The panel gave no percentage.
  - Intensity can be lower, the same, or still high if volume drops (76-95% agreement). The panel did not mandate an intensity cut.
  - Timing: deloads can be pre-planned (100%), autoregulatory (100%) or both (100%). Deload "when athletes feel fatigued, regardless of mesocycle week" (100%). The athlete may schedule deloads (95.2%). Frequency depends on athlete response and other sport training (100%).
  - The panel reached no consensus on deload length. The "~7 days" and "every 4-6 weeks" figures come from the paper's literature review and coaching practice [EO], not from panel consensus.
- [RCT] Coleman et al. 2024 (PeerJ 12:e16777), "Gaining more from doing less?" Design: 39 resistance-trained adults (≥1 year, ≥3 sessions/week), 9 weeks, upper/lower split 4 days/week, 5 sets × 8-12RM close to failure, ~90 sets/week. The deload group did no resistance training at all in week 5. — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)
  - Hypertrophy (ultrasound) was the same in both groups. Continuous training was favoured for isometric knee extension strength (posterior probability 0.92) and slightly for Smith squat 1RM. The deload produced no "resensitisation" effect. Continuous trainees reported slightly better readiness: less soreness and no dip in motivation.
  - Authors: deloads by training cessation "should be used sparingly when maximizing strength". With short programs at ≤90 sets/week, young trainees may not need deloads. The study tested only full cessation and a pre-planned deload, not a reduced-volume one.
- [Trial] Ogasawara et al. (ACSM 2010 presentation): untrained men did bench press at 75% 1RM, 3 days/week, for 15 weeks. One group took a 3-week break at weeks 7-9. Strength was maintained during the break. Retraining gains matched the initial phase, and final gains equalled continuous training, whose gains in weeks 10-15 had slowed. — [CoachSci summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol171/ogasawar.htm)

### Inferences
- For this user, the evidence supports reactive deloads, triggered by a stall or by fatigue, over calendar deloads. The Delphi panel explicitly endorses autoregulated deloads "regardless of mesocycle week". An irregular 1-6 days/week schedule also produces natural deloads, because low-frequency weeks are de facto volume reductions. Concrete implementable rule (convention, not trial-validated):
  1. Stall rule per exercise: if the target reps at the current load are missed in 3 consecutive exposures, reduce that exercise's load by ~10% and rebuild. This is the openGym/StrongLifts-style reset [EO].
  2. Global fatigue rule: if ≥2 main lifts stall at the same time, or session readiness is low twice in a row (see Q3), run one deload session block. Use the same exercises, about 50% of the sets, and the same or slightly lower load (~-10%) at ≥3-4 RIR. Do this for the next 2-4 sessions, or about 1 week of training. The volume-first cut follows the Delphi consensus; the 50% figure is convention [EO].
  3. Don't schedule full-cessation deloads. The RCT suggests a small strength cost, and a kung fu teaching schedule already provides variation.
- A gap in the queue of more than ~2-4 weeks should be treated as a deload / re-entry rather than adding an extra deload (see Q4).

### Gaps
- No RCT has compared a reduced-volume deload with continuous training or with a cessation deload. No study has tested stall-triggered vs calendar deloads. The "3 stalls → -10%" rule is coaching convention with no trial data.
- No validated optimal deload frequency exists for intermediates.

## Q3. Fatigue and readiness monitoring: which markers are validated, and does readiness-based adjustment help?

### Takeaway
For lifters, the best-supported practical markers are performance-based: RIR/RPE-based load regulation, bar velocity, and simple performance tests such as jump height. Wellness questionnaires and HRV are used widely but have weak strength-specific evidence. An app can rely on RIR logging and an e1RM trend.

### Cited Findings
- [SR] Helms et al. 2020 (J Hum Kinet 74:23-42), "Methods for Regulating and Monitoring Resistance Training". The review covered 90 studies and 11 themes. Sessional RIR-based RPE and post-set Borg RPE were strongly associated with training performance, and RIR-based RPE is "practical and reliable" for modifying intensity and volume. Barbell velocity was strongly associated with performance, and velocity-based modification has preliminary evidence of benefit. Jump height and throw distance are "low-risk stand-ins for maximal strength tests". Some measures correlated at r ≥ 0.68 with RT performance. Blood markers, EMG and force plates are logistically restrictive. — [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/b67c0527-5e33-45d9-886c-746d3a659ea0/full)
- [MA] Autoregulation using RPE alone (2 studies) showed SMD 0.17, not significant, while APRE (performance-based rep adjustment) showed SMD 0.78. — [Zhang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- [RCT] In Coleman 2024, self-reported readiness (soreness, motivation) differed only modestly between groups, which shows subjective readiness scales pick up deload effects only weakly. — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)

### Inferences
- Rules an app can implement:
  - Log reps and RIR for the top set. Compute e1RM, e.g. Epley adjusted for RIR: e1RM = load × (1 + (reps + RIR)/30).
  - A performance-based fatigue flag: e1RM on a main lift falls >5-7% below its rolling best of the last 3 exposures, or the same load feels ≥2 RPE harder than last time. On a flag, cut that session's sets by ~1/3 or hold load. This works like APRE / day-to-day autoregulation [EO thresholds].
  - An optional one-tap pre-session check (sleep, soreness especially legs after kung fu, motivation, 1-5 each). Use it only to pick the lighter session in the queue or trim volume, never to raise load [EO].
- HRV is not worth building in for a strength-only app. The evidence reached does not support it for strength, and it needs daily wearable measurement that irregular users rarely keep up.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of HRV-guided strength training was found within this research; the evidence on HRV for strength is unknown here rather than shown to be negative.
- I could not retrieve validation data for subjective wellness questionnaires (e.g. Hooper index) in lifters specifically.
- Precise RIR-prediction accuracy figures (e.g. Zourdos/Helms work showing better accuracy near failure) were not retrieved.

## Q4. Irregular frequency, detraining, maintenance dose and "muscle memory": when to set a baseline after a break

### Takeaway
Maximal strength barely changes for about 3-4 weeks off. One session a week at the same relative intensity maintains strength for months. Strength lost after a break comes back in roughly a third to two-thirds of the break's length. A "baseline" should therefore be set only after the regain phase has flattened, not in the first sessions back.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Bosquet et al. 2013 (103 studies): maximal strength was largely unaffected up to ~28 days of training cessation (g < 0.2), and losses accelerated after that. Strength endurance dropped earlier and more. Adults ≥65 lost strength about twice as fast (g 0.76 vs 0.31). — [Stronger by Science, detraining](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/) (secondary summary; original via [EHU record](https://ekoizpen-zientifikoa.ehu.eus/documentos/5f1cda5129995265e44d37a4?lang=en))
- [SR/MA] Encarnação et al. 2022 (Muscles 1(1):1-15), 20 studies. 1RM fell substantially during detraining but stayed above pre-training baseline at 16-24 weeks (chest press g +1.99, leg press +3.16). At 32 and 48 weeks it was no longer significantly above baseline. Study quality was low (PEDro 2-6). — [MDPI](https://www2.mdpi.com/2813-0413/1/1/1)
- [RCT] Bickel et al. 2011 (MSSE 43(7):1177-87), n=70. After 16 weeks at 3 days/week (3×8-12), 32 weeks of maintenance at 1 day/week with 1/3 or 1/9 of the volume preserved strength and even raised it 7-8%. Full detraining lost ~7% strength over 32 weeks. Young adults kept muscle size with 1/9 of the volume (1 set per exercise, once a week), while older adults did not. — [Bickel 2011 PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Exercise%20Dosing%20to%20Retain%20Resistance%20Training%20Adaptations%20in%20Young%20and%20Older%20Adults.pdf)
- [SR] Spiering et al. 2021 (JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964). In younger people, strength and size can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with 1 session/week and 1 set per exercise, as long as relative load (intensity) is kept. Older people may need 2 sessions/week and 2-3 sets. Intensity is the key variable. — [Fisiología del Ejercicio summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/que-dosis-minima-se-necesita-para-mantener-la-fuerza-y-la-resistencia-aerobica-a-traves-del-tiempo/) (blog summary; original abstract not retrieved)
- [Trial] Ogasawara: a 3-week break kept strength, and retraining caught up fully. — [CoachSci](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol171/ogasawar.htm)
- [Trial] Psilander et al. (2019): 10 weeks training, 20 weeks detraining, 5 weeks retraining. Subjects ended slightly stronger and more muscular than at the end of the original training. — [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/) (secondary)
- [EO] Nuckols (Stronger by Science) heuristics:
  - Regain time ≈ half the break (range 1/3-2/3).
  - Breaks under 1 month: reduce loads ~20% in week 1.
  - Longer breaks, week 1: use normal exercises and volume at ~1/3 of prior loads.
  - Longer breaks, week 2: challenging but ≥5 RIR on the first set and 3-4 on the last.
  - Then add (previous working weight − current) / (estimated weeks − 1) per week.
  - Breaks over 1 year: treat yourself as a novice with linear progression.
  - — [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)
- [MA] Volume-equated 1 vs ≥3 days/week gave similar strength. — [Sports Med Open 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)

### Inferences
- Irregular frequency (1-6 days/week) is not harmful in itself, as long as weekly hard sets per lift and relative intensity are kept over a rolling window. Gaps under ~3 weeks cost almost no maximal strength. Implementable rules:
  - **Gap-aware load**:
    - Last exposure to a lift ≤14 days ago: proceed as normal.
    - 15-28 days: repeat the last load (no increment) or drop ~5%.
    - 4-8 weeks: drop ~10-20%.
    - Longer: use the return-to-training ramp. These thresholds are derived from Bosquet and Nuckols [EO].
  - **Minimum dose**: if a week is only 1 session, keep the heavy sets (intensity) and cut accessory volume. That keeps strength per Bickel/Spiering.
- **Baseline after return** (the user restarted Sept 2026): during regain, progress is faster than true intermediate progression. Strength returns in roughly 1/3-2/3 of the break length, and Ogasawara's retraining matched initial gains. Suggested rule:
  - Run fast linear progression, with small fixed increments every exposure while all reps hit at ≥2 RIR.
  - Declare "baseline" per lift when either (a) the first stall/miss occurs, or (b) e1RM gain per exposure falls below ~1% for 3 consecutive exposures, or (c) after about half the break length has elapsed. Use whichever comes first.
  - Then set the baseline = best e1RM of the last 3 exposures, and start the stall/deload rules (Q2) only from that point.
  - Earlier stalls are better handled by repeating the load than by a 10% reset.
  - These rules are an inference from the evidence above, not a tested protocol.

### Gaps
- Bosquet 2013 and Psilander 2019 numbers come from a secondary source. The primary texts were blocked or robots-disallowed.
- No study tests detraining in well-trained intermediates who keep a different sport going, such as kung fu (partial bodyweight/leg loading may slow losses). Not found.
- No study validates a "baseline-setting" criterion after return.

## Q5. Concurrent training interference: martial arts / high-intensity skill training plus strength

### Takeaway
Meta-analytic evidence for aerobic plus strength training shows no interference with maximal strength or hypertrophy, but some loss of explosive strength, mainly when both are done in the same session. Separating sessions by ≥3 h removes this effect. Direct evidence for martial arts is lacking.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52(3):601-612), 43 studies, 1,090 participants. Concurrent vs strength-only:
  - maximal strength SMD -0.06 (NS)
  - hypertrophy -0.01 (NS)
  - explosive strength -0.28 (P=0.007)
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- [MA] Schumann 2022 moderators: the explosive-strength interference appeared when both were done in the same session, ≤20 min apart (SMD -0.31), and not when sessions were ≥3 h apart. Session order, running vs cycling, frequency, training status and age were not significant moderators. The explosive finding was sensitive to one outlier study, and mean PEDro was 4.3/10. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)

### Inferences
- Kung fu (4 nights/week, mixed aerobic/anaerobic, kicks and stances) is closest to "concurrent aerobic + power" training. The meta-analysis suggests max strength is safe. Explosive qualities, which matter to a martial artist, are better protected with these scheduling rules [EO extrapolation]:
  - Lift ≥3 h before kung fu, or on another day.
  - Avoid heavy or high-volume lower-body work in the hours before kicking or sparring, both for skill quality and for injury risk.
  - Put the heaviest lower-body session in the queue on non-kung-fu days, or in the morning when possible.
  - Count kung fu nights toward weekly fatigue. Two or more consecutive kung fu nights plus a leg-heavy session can be a soft trigger for the lighter queue session.
- Kung fu is a fixed weekly load. The gym's weekly volume should therefore be moderate, e.g. around 10 hard sets/muscle/week is a common evidence-based coaching midpoint [EO; not sourced here]. Exceeding it would add fatigue without much strength benefit.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis or RCT was found on martial-arts training combined with resistance training and strength outcomes. Schumann covers aerobic running and cycling only.
- No data was found on the residual fatigue of martial-arts sessions (e.g. CMJ drop hours after training).

## Q6. Fitness-fatigue (Banister impulse-response) model: validated and practical for an individual?

### Takeaway
The Banister model is conceptually standard: performance = fitness - fatigue, each with its own decay. For resistance training, though, its fitting is fragile, it needs frequent precise performance measures, and its predictive validity is unestablished. It is not practical as a planning engine for an irregular individual lifter, though a simplified two-EWMA "fitness vs fatigue" display is a reasonable heuristic.

### Cited Findings
- [Thesis] Stephens Hemingway 2021 (PhD, Robert Gordon University):
  - Model structure: introduced by Banister et al. 1975. Each training bout creates fitness (long-lasting, low magnitude) and fatigue (short-lasting, high magnitude). The standard form has 5 free parameters and needs quantified daily training doses plus frequent performance measures.
  - Validity: prior research often did not test validity adequately.
  - Fitting: in simulation, high measurement error and infrequent testing produce unsatisfactory fits. The L-BFGS-B fitting algorithm is sensitive to starting points and local optima, so the fitting problem is harder than acknowledged and may need global optimisation.
  - Better inputs: barbell velocity, session RPE, RIR or %1RM make the data more informative.
  - Conclusion: the model has potential, but its validity and practical utility remain unestablished.
  — [RGU repository](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1603170)

### Inferences
- An app can compute two exponentially weighted moving averages of training load, e.g. tonnage × relative intensity, or hard sets × %e1RM, with time constants of roughly 42 days (fitness) and 7 days (fatigue), the classic ACWR/Banister-style defaults [EO]. It can show "form = fitness - fatigue" as an informational readiness hint. It should not fit individual parameters or prescribe loads from it, because irregular sessions and noisy e1RM make the fit unreliable.

### Gaps
- No validated individual resistance-training Banister parameter values or predictive-accuracy figures were retrieved.
- The thesis's later studies (real-athlete data) were beyond the retrieved excerpt.
